# Graduate CV Example (2026): Recruiter-Approved Template and Writing Guide

Graduating from university or TVET is an exciting milestone, but it also introduces a new challenge.

Many graduates ask the same question:

> **How do I compete for professional jobs when I have limited experience?**

The answer is not to exaggerate your qualifications or fill your CV with generic buzzwords.

Recruiters hiring graduates understand that most applicants are at the beginning of their careers.

Instead of expecting years of professional experience, they look for evidence that you have developed the knowledge, skills and potential needed to succeed in an entry-level role.

Your internships, industrial attachment, academic projects, leadership positions, volunteer work and certifications all help tell that story.

A well-written graduate CV brings these experiences together in a clear, professional and relevant way.

This guide includes a recruiter-approved graduate CV example together with detailed explanations of every section so that you understand not only what to include, but why recruiters expect to see it.

Whether you are applying for a graduate trainee programme, an entry-level position or your first professional job, this guide will help you prepare a stronger application.

---

## Quick Answer

A strong graduate CV highlights internships, academic qualifications, projects, transferable skills and measurable achievements.

Recruiters are looking for evidence that you can transition successfully into the workplace—not that you already have years of professional experience.

---

## Who This Guide Is For

This guide is suitable for:

- Recent university graduates.
- TVET graduates.
- Graduate trainee applicants.
- First-time job seekers.
- Candidates with internship experience.
- Candidates returning to the job market after completing their studies.

If you are preparing your first professional CV after graduation, this guide is designed for you.

---

## What Recruiters Expect From Graduate CVs

Graduate recruitment differs from hiring experienced professionals.

Recruiters understand that graduates are still developing practical workplace experience.

Instead, they assess indicators such as:

- Academic performance.
- Internship or industrial attachment experience.
- Problem-solving ability.
- Communication skills.
- Initiative.
- Adaptability.
- Leadership potential.
- Professional presentation.

They are asking one important question:

> **Does this candidate demonstrate the potential to succeed and grow within our organisation?**

Every section of your CV should help answer that question.

> **Recruiter Insight**
>
> Graduates are not expected to know everything on day one. Recruiters value curiosity, learning ability and professionalism just as much as technical knowledge.

## Why This Graduate CV Works

Although the candidate is early in their career, the CV demonstrates readiness for professional employment.

Notice that it:

- Opens with a targeted professional summary.
- Highlights internship experience before projects.
- Focuses on measurable responsibilities.
- Demonstrates technical competence.
- Includes relevant certifications.
- Uses a clean, ATS-friendly structure.
- Avoids unnecessary personal information.

Most importantly, every section supports the candidate's suitability for an entry-level software development role rather than simply listing academic achievements.

## Section 1: Contact Information

Your contact information should be accurate, professional and easy to find.

Include:

- Full name
- Mobile phone number
- Professional email address
- County or city of residence
- LinkedIn profile (recommended if updated)

Example:

```text
DAVID OTIENO OUMA

Nairobi, Kenya

+254 712 345 678

david.otieno@example.com

linkedin.com/in/davidotieno
```

Avoid including:

- National ID number
- Date of birth (unless specifically requested)
- Marital status
- Religion
- Gender
- Passport photograph unless required

These details rarely influence hiring decisions and occupy valuable space that could be used to showcase your qualifications.

> **Recruiter Insight**
>
> A recruiter should be able to identify you and contact you within seconds of opening your CV.

---

## Section 2: Professional Summary

Your professional summary introduces you as a graduate entering the workforce.

It should briefly explain:

- Your qualification.
- Your strongest technical or professional skills.
- Relevant internship or project experience.
- The type of opportunity you are seeking.

The example summary works because it immediately communicates:

- Academic background.
- Practical internship experience.
- Technical competence.
- Career objective.

Avoid generic introductions such as:

> "I am a hardworking graduate looking for an opportunity to grow."

Instead, demonstrate value from the very beginning.

---

## Section 3: Education

Education remains an important section for graduates, but unlike student CVs, it no longer needs to dominate the document.

Include:

- Institution.
- Degree or diploma.
- Graduation date.
- Academic honours (if relevant).
- Relevant coursework only if it strengthens your application.

Example:

```text
Jomo Kenyatta University of Agriculture and Technology

Bachelor of Science in Computer Science

Graduated:
December 2025
```

As your professional experience grows, education gradually becomes less prominent.

---

## Section 4: Internship Experience

Internships and industrial attachment often become the strongest section of a graduate CV.

Recruiters understand that this may be your first exposure to a professional working environment.

Instead of simply listing duties, explain your contribution.

Example:

```text
Software Development Intern

ABC Technologies Ltd

May 2025 – August 2025

• Assisted in developing internal web applications.

• Supported testing and debugging activities.

• Improved SQL reporting queries.

• Worked alongside senior software engineers.
```

This demonstrates practical experience without exaggerating responsibilities.

### If You Did Not Complete an Internship

Do not panic.

Instead, strengthen other sections such as:

- Academic projects.
- Freelance work.
- Volunteer experience.
- Personal portfolio projects.
- Open-source contributions.
- Research.

Recruiters assess the complete picture—not only internships.

---

## Section 5: Projects

Projects demonstrate your ability to apply classroom knowledge to practical problems.

Strong projects include:

- Final-year projects.
- Software applications.
- Business case studies.
- Engineering designs.
- Research projects.
- Data analysis work.

Instead of simply naming the project, explain:

- The objective.
- Your contribution.
- The technologies or methods used.
- The outcome.

For example:

```text
Student Management System

• Developed a React-based web application.

• Designed a PostgreSQL database.

• Collaborated using Git version control.

• Presented the solution during final-year project assessment.
```

Recruiters are interested in what you accomplished—not just the project title.

---

## Section 6: Technical Skills

Graduates often underestimate the importance of a focused skills section.

List skills that are:

- Relevant to the role.
- Current.
- Verifiable.

Examples include:

### Technology

- Java
- Python
- JavaScript
- React
- SQL
- Git
- AWS

### Business

- Microsoft Excel
- Power BI
- Financial Modelling
- Data Analysis
- SAP

### Engineering

- AutoCAD
- MATLAB
- SolidWorks

Avoid listing software or technologies you cannot confidently discuss during an interview.

> **Career Coach Tip**
>
> If a recruiter asks you to demonstrate a skill you've listed, you should be comfortable explaining how you've used it in a project, internship or coursework.

---

## Section 7: Certifications

Professional certifications demonstrate initiative beyond formal education.

Examples include:

- Google Career Certificates
- Microsoft Learn
- AWS Academy
- Cisco Networking Academy
- HubSpot Academy
- Oracle Academy
- IBM SkillsBuild
- Coursera Specialisations

For each certification, include:

- Name.
- Issuing organisation.
- Completion year (optional if recent).

Prioritise certifications that strengthen your application for the role.

---

## Section 8: Referees

Many employers no longer require referees during the first stage of recruitment.

If they are not requested, simply include:

> Referees available upon request.

If specific referees are requested, choose people who know your work professionally or academically, such as:

- Internship supervisors.
- Lecturers.
- Project supervisors.
- Volunteer coordinators.
- Previous employers.

Always obtain their permission before sharing their contact details.

## Common Graduate CV Mistakes

Graduating from university or TVET is a significant achievement, but many graduates reduce their chances of securing interviews because of avoidable CV mistakes.

Understanding these mistakes can help you present yourself more effectively.

### Treating Your University CV as Your Professional CV

Many graduates simply update the student CV they used for internships.

While this may seem convenient, recruiters expect graduates to demonstrate greater professional readiness.

Your graduate CV should place greater emphasis on:

- Internship experience.
- Practical projects.
- Technical skills.
- Certifications.
- Professional achievements.

As you gain experience, your CV should evolve with you.

---

### Focusing on Responsibilities Instead of Results

Graduates often write:

- Assisted with customer service.
- Participated in software development.
- Supported accounting activities.

These statements describe duties but provide little insight into your contribution.

Instead, explain what you achieved.

For example:

- Resolved customer enquiries while maintaining a high level of client satisfaction.
- Developed new application features as part of a four-person development team.
- Automated monthly financial reporting using Microsoft Excel.

Recruiters are interested in outcomes as much as responsibilities.

---

### Listing Every Skill You Have Ever Learned

A long list of software and technical skills does not necessarily strengthen your application.

Recruiters expect your skills to match the role.

Prioritise:

- Skills requested in the vacancy.
- Skills you can confidently explain.
- Skills supported by projects or experience.

Quality is more persuasive than quantity.

---

### Ignoring Professional Development

Many graduates stop learning after graduation.

However, employers value candidates who continue developing their knowledge.

Examples include:

- Professional certifications.
- Online courses.
- Industry workshops.
- Technical portfolios.
- Open-source contributions.
- Personal projects.

Continuous learning demonstrates initiative and adaptability.

---

### Sending the Same CV Everywhere

A graduate trainee programme, software engineering role and banking position all require different strengths.

Tailor your CV by:

- Updating the professional summary.
- Reordering projects.
- Highlighting relevant technical skills.
- Prioritising the most relevant internship experience.

Tailoring demonstrates genuine interest in the opportunity.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## Should My Graduate CV Still Include University Projects?

Yes.

Projects remain valuable, especially if they demonstrate technical ability, research, teamwork or problem-solving.

As your career progresses, professional achievements will naturally become more important.

---

## Is Internship Experience Enough for My First Job?

Absolutely.

Recruiters understand that internships are often a graduate's first exposure to the workplace.

Focus on explaining what you learned, contributed and achieved.

---

## How Long Should a Graduate CV Be?

One page is appropriate for most recent graduates.

If you have extensive internships, research projects or relevant professional experience, a second page may be justified.

Every section should add value.

---

## Should I Include My CGPA?

Include your CGPA if:

- It is strong.
- The employer requests it.
- It strengthens your application.

If it does not add value, it is not mandatory.

---

## What If My Degree Is Not Directly Related to the Job?

Focus on transferable skills.

Highlight experiences that demonstrate:

- Communication.
- Leadership.
- Problem-solving.
- Analytical thinking.
- Technical ability.
- Adaptability.

Employers often hire graduates for their potential to learn and grow.

---

# Key Takeaways

A graduate CV should demonstrate readiness for professional employment—not years of experience.

Remember these principles:

- Tailor every application.
- Highlight internships and projects.
- Support skills with evidence.
- Keep formatting clean and professional.
- Continue building your qualifications after graduation.
- Demonstrate initiative through continuous learning.
- Focus on achievements rather than responsibilities.
- Proofread carefully before every submission.

Recruiters know that every experienced professional once applied for their first graduate role.

Your objective is to show that you have the foundation, professionalism and willingness to succeed.

# Final Thoughts

Your first graduate CV is not expected to compete with someone who has ten years of experience.

Every internship, project, certification and leadership opportunity contributes to that story.

Present those experiences honestly, organise them clearly and tailor your CV for every application.

A professional, well-structured graduate CV will not guarantee an interview, but it gives recruiters the information they need to recognise your potential and invite you to the next stage of the recruitment process.
