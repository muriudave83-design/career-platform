# Student CV Example (2026): Recruiter-Approved Template and Writing Guide

Writing your first CV can feel overwhelming.

Many students worry that they do not have enough experience to impress employers. Others are unsure what to include, what to leave out or how to organise their information professionally.

The good news is that recruiters do not expect students to have years of work experience.

Instead, they look for potential, relevant skills, academic achievements, projects, leadership experience and evidence that you can learn quickly and contribute effectively.

A well-written student CV highlights these strengths clearly and professionally.

This guide includes a recruiter-approved student CV example together with detailed explanations of every section, helping you understand not only **what** to write but also **why** each section matters.

Whether you are applying for internships, industrial attachment, graduate trainee programmes, part-time jobs or your first full-time position, the principles in this guide will help you create a stronger CV.

---

## Quick Answer

A strong student CV focuses on education, projects, leadership, volunteer experience, technical skills and achievements rather than extensive work history.

Recruiters understand that students have limited professional experience. They are looking for evidence of potential, initiative and relevant skills—not a long employment history.

---

## Who This Guide Is For

This guide is designed for:

- University students.
- TVET students.
- Recent graduates.
- Internship applicants.
- Industrial attachment applicants.
- Graduate trainee applicants.
- Students applying for scholarships or exchange programmes.

If you have little or no formal work experience, this guide will help you present your strengths confidently.

---

## Before You Start Writing

Before opening a blank document, spend a few minutes gathering the information you may need.

This includes:

- Your current contact details.
- Academic qualifications.
- Projects.
- Leadership positions.
- Volunteer activities.
- Technical skills.
- Certifications.
- Awards or achievements.
- Referees (if requested).

Collecting this information first makes writing your CV much easier.

---

## What Recruiters Expect From Student CVs

Recruiters reviewing student applications understand that you are at the beginning of your career.

They are not expecting years of employment.

Instead, they typically assess:

- Academic performance.
- Relevant coursework.
- Practical projects.
- Communication skills.
- Leadership potential.
- Willingness to learn.
- Professional presentation.
- Attention to detail.

Your goal is to demonstrate that you have the foundation to succeed, even if your professional experience is still developing.

> **Recruiter Insight**
>
> Students often underestimate the value of university projects, leadership roles and volunteer work. These experiences frequently demonstrate transferable skills that employers actively seek.

## Why This CV Works

Although the candidate has no full-time professional experience, the CV demonstrates qualities that recruiters value.

Notice how it:

- Uses a clean, professional layout.
- Begins with a relevant professional summary.
- Highlights education early.
- Includes academic projects.
- Demonstrates leadership.
- Shows volunteer involvement.
- Lists relevant technical skills.
- Avoids unnecessary personal information.

Most importantly, every section contributes to the candidate's suitability for an internship or graduate opportunity.
---

## Section 1: Contact Information

Your contact information should be the simplest part of your CV, but mistakes here can prevent recruiters from contacting you.

Include:

- Full name
- Phone number
- Professional email address
- County or city of residence
- LinkedIn profile (optional but recommended if complete)

For example:

```text
MARY NJERI MWANGI

Nairobi, Kenya
+254 712 345 678
mary.njeri@example.com
linkedin.com/in/marynjeri
```

Avoid including:

- National ID number
- Date of birth (unless specifically requested)
- Marital status
- Religion
- Gender
- Passport-sized photographs unless the employer asks for one

These details rarely influence hiring decisions and unnecessarily occupy valuable space.

> **Recruiter Insight**
>
> The easiest CV to review is one where recruiters can immediately identify the candidate and know how to make contact.

---

## Section 2: Professional Summary

Your professional summary introduces you to the recruiter.

It should explain:

- Who you are.
- What you are studying.
- Your strongest skills.
- What type of opportunity you are seeking.

Notice the example:

> Final-year Bachelor of Commerce student at the University of Nairobi with strong analytical, communication and problem-solving skills developed through academic projects and student leadership. Seeking an internship opportunity to apply financial analysis, Microsoft Excel and research skills while gaining practical industry experience.

This summary works because it is:

- Specific.
- Relevant.
- Honest.
- Tailored to internship applications.

Avoid generic statements such as:

> I am hardworking, self-motivated and willing to learn.

These phrases describe many applicants and do little to distinguish you.

---

## Section 3: Education

For most students, education should appear near the top of the CV.

Include:

- Institution name.
- Course.
- Expected graduation date.
- Relevant coursework (where appropriate).
- Academic achievements if they strengthen your application.

Example:

```text
University of Nairobi

Bachelor of Commerce (Finance)

Expected Graduation:
December 2026

Relevant Coursework

• Financial Accounting
• Corporate Finance
• Business Statistics
• Investment Analysis
```

### Should You Include Your KCSE Results?

If you are still early in your university or TVET programme and have limited additional experience, including your KCSE grades can provide useful context.

However, as you gain internships, projects and professional experience, your tertiary education and practical achievements should take greater prominence.

---

## Section 4: Academic Projects

Projects are often underestimated by students.

Recruiters recognise that practical coursework demonstrates valuable workplace skills.

Good projects show:

- Research.
- Teamwork.
- Problem-solving.
- Presentation skills.
- Technical ability.
- Initiative.

Rather than simply listing a project title, explain what you actually did.

Instead of:

> Financial Analysis Project

Write:

```text
Financial Analysis Project

• Analysed financial statements for a listed Kenyan company.
• Calculated profitability and liquidity ratios.
• Presented recommendations during a group presentation.
```

Recruiters are interested in your contribution—not just the assignment name.

---

## Section 5: Leadership Experience

Leadership is not limited to student government.

You may have gained leadership experience as:

- Class representative.
- Club official.
- Sports captain.
- Event organiser.
- Peer mentor.
- Committee member.

Focus on responsibilities that demonstrate initiative and collaboration.

For example:

```text
Class Representative

• Represented more than 80 students during academic meetings.
• Coordinated communication between students and lecturers.
• Organised class discussions before examinations.
```

This demonstrates communication, organisation and responsibility.

---

## Section 6: Volunteer Experience

Volunteer work is valuable because it shows initiative beyond academic requirements.

Examples include:

- Community outreach.
- Charity events.
- Environmental programmes.
- Youth organisations.
- Faith-based initiatives.
- Campus volunteering.

Recruiters often associate volunteer experience with:

- Reliability.
- Teamwork.
- Community engagement.
- Leadership potential.

If your volunteer role involved measurable responsibilities, describe them using concise bullet points.

> **Career Coach Tip**
>
> Do not underestimate volunteer work. Many employers value practical initiative just as much as paid experience when recruiting students.

---

## Section 7: Technical Skills

Your skills section should reinforce—not replace—the rest of your CV.

List skills you can confidently demonstrate if asked during an interview.

Examples include:

- Microsoft Excel
- Microsoft Word
- PowerPoint
- Google Workspace
- Data Analysis
- Financial Modelling
- AutoCAD
- Python
- Java
- SQL
- Adobe Photoshop

Only include software or technical skills you have genuinely used.

Recruiters may ask practical questions to verify them.

---

## Section 8: Certifications

Professional certifications strengthen student CVs because they demonstrate initiative outside formal education.

Examples include:

- Google Digital Skills
- Microsoft Learn certifications
- Cisco Networking Academy
- AWS Academy
- HubSpot Academy
- LinkedIn Learning
- Alison
- Coursera (where relevant)

List:

- Certification name.
- Issuing organisation.
- Completion year (if appropriate).

Even introductory certifications can strengthen internship applications when they relate to the position.

---

## Section 9: Referees

Many employers no longer require referees during the initial application stage.

If instructions do not request them, you may simply write:

> Referees available upon request.

If referees are requested, ensure they:

- Know you professionally or academically.
- Have agreed to act as your referee.
- Can speak positively about your abilities.

Never include someone's contact information without their permission.

## Common Student CV Mistakes

Many students believe their biggest disadvantage is having limited work experience.

In reality, recruiters are often more concerned about **how** a CV is presented than the amount of experience it contains.

Avoid these common mistakes.

### Using a Generic CV for Every Application

Different employers value different skills.

Tailor your professional summary, projects and highlighted skills to match each vacancy.

A customised CV demonstrates genuine interest and makes your application more relevant.

---

### Writing a Weak Professional Summary

Your summary should explain why you are a suitable candidate.

Avoid vague statements such as:

> "I am a hardworking student looking for an opportunity to grow."

Instead, mention:

- Your course.
- Your strongest technical skills.
- Relevant projects.
- The opportunity you are seeking.

---

### Ignoring Academic Projects

Many students leave projects off their CV because they were completed as coursework.

This is a missed opportunity.

Projects often demonstrate:

- Research
- Analysis
- Teamwork
- Technical ability
- Communication
- Problem-solving

These are exactly the qualities employers look for in students.

---

### Listing Skills Without Evidence

If your skills section says:

- Leadership
- Teamwork
- Communication

make sure another section of your CV demonstrates those skills.

For example:

- Leadership through class representative duties.
- Communication through presentations.
- Teamwork through group projects.

Evidence always carries more weight than claims.

---

### Poor Formatting

Student CVs should be simple and easy to scan.

Avoid:

- Multiple fonts.
- Bright colours.
- Decorative graphics.
- Excessive borders.
- Long paragraphs.

Professional formatting helps recruiters focus on your qualifications instead of your layout.

---

## Adapting Your CV for Different Opportunities

One student CV should not be used for every application.

Instead, adjust your emphasis depending on the opportunity.

### Internship Applications

Highlight:

- Relevant coursework.
- Academic projects.
- Technical skills.
- Willingness to learn.

Recruiters understand that internships are learning opportunities.

---

### Graduate Trainee Programmes

Emphasise:

- Leadership.
- Analytical ability.
- Communication.
- Academic achievements.
- Internships and projects.

Graduate programmes often seek candidates with strong potential rather than extensive experience.

---

### Part-Time Jobs

Focus on transferable skills such as:

- Customer service.
- Time management.
- Reliability.
- Teamwork.
- Communication.

Volunteer work and campus responsibilities can strengthen these applications.

---

### Scholarships and Exchange Programmes

Highlight:

- Academic performance.
- Leadership.
- Community service.
- Research.
- Extracurricular achievements.

The same CV structure can be adapted while changing the emphasis to suit the opportunity.

## Recruiter's Student CV Checklist

Before submitting your application, ask yourself:

| Question | Yes / No |
|-----------|-----------|
| Is my contact information correct? | ☐ |
| Does my professional summary match the opportunity? | ☐ |
| Is my education clearly presented? | ☐ |
| Have I included relevant projects? | ☐ |
| Have I demonstrated leadership or teamwork? | ☐ |
| Are my technical skills accurate? | ☐ |
| Is the formatting clean and consistent? | ☐ |
| Have I proofread the document? | ☐ |
| Have I tailored this CV for the employer? | ☐ |
| Would I feel confident discussing everything included? | ☐ |

If every answer is **Yes**, your CV is ready for submission.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## What If I Have Never Had a Job?

That is completely normal.

Use academic projects, leadership positions, volunteer work, competitions, certifications and technical skills to demonstrate your potential.

Recruiters expect students to have limited professional experience.

---

## Should I Include My KCSE Results?

If you are applying early in your university or TVET studies and have little additional experience, they can provide useful context.

As your tertiary education, internships and projects grow, they become less important.

---

## How Long Should a Student CV Be?

For most students, one page is ideal.

Only extend to two pages if you have enough relevant experience to justify the additional length.

Quality is more important than quantity.

---

## Do I Need a Cover Letter?

Many employers request one.

A well-written cover letter complements your CV by explaining your interest in the role and how your skills align with the employer's needs.

---

## Should I Include Hobbies?

Only if they strengthen your application or demonstrate transferable skills.

Examples include:

- Debate.
- Robotics.
- Coding.
- Community volunteering.
- Sports leadership.

Avoid listing hobbies that add little professional value.

---

# Key Takeaways

A strong student CV focuses on potential rather than years of experience.

Remember these principles:

- Present information clearly.
- Highlight education early.
- Include meaningful projects.
- Demonstrate leadership and initiative.
- Support skills with evidence.
- Tailor every application.
- Keep formatting professional.
- Proofread before submitting.

Recruiters understand that every experienced professional was once a student.

Your goal is not to compete with experienced candidates—it is to show that you have the knowledge, initiative and potential to succeed.
