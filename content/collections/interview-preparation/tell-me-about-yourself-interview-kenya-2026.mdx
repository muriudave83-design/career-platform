# Tell Me About Yourself in an Interview (Kenya 2026): Best Answers with Examples

Few interview questions make candidates more nervous than:

> **"Tell me about yourself."**

Although it sounds simple, this opening question often determines the tone of the entire interview.

Many candidates respond by reciting their CV, sharing personal information that isn't relevant or speaking for several minutes without answering the question directly.

As a result, they miss an important opportunity to make a strong first impression.

The good news is that this question is highly predictable.

With the right preparation, you can answer confidently, highlight your strengths and guide the conversation towards your most relevant experiences.

Whether you're applying for an internship, graduate trainee programme, entry-level role or an experienced position, the principles remain largely the same.

In this guide, you'll learn:

- why recruiters ask this question
- what they're really assessing
- a simple framework for building a strong answer
- examples for different career stages
- common mistakes to avoid
- practical tips to help you deliver your answer naturally

## Quick Answer

When an interviewer asks, **"Tell me about yourself,"** they are not asking for your life story.

They want a concise introduction that explains:

- who you are professionally
- your relevant education or experience
- your key strengths
- why you're interested in the role

A useful structure is:

1. Your current situation
2. Your relevant background
3. Your key strengths or achievements
4. Why you're excited about this opportunity

Most strong answers take **between one and two minutes**.

The goal is to create interest, establish credibility and prepare the interviewer for the rest of the conversation.

---

# Why Interviewers Ask "Tell Me About Yourself"

This question serves several purposes.

It helps interviewers evaluate more than just the content of your answer.

They're also observing **how** you answer.

During your response, recruiters assess:

- communication skills
- confidence
- organisation
- professionalism
- self-awareness
- ability to prioritise information

Your answer also helps interviewers understand how you see yourself professionally.

Candidates who communicate clearly and stay focused often create a stronger first impression than those who provide long, unstructured introductions.

> **Recruiter's Perspective**
>
> This question isn't designed to catch candidates off guard. It's an opportunity to settle into the interview, demonstrate confidence and highlight the experiences most relevant to the role.

---

# The Biggest Mistake Candidates Make

One of the most common misconceptions is believing that "Tell me about yourself" is an invitation to discuss your entire personal history.

Interviewers are not looking for:

- where you were born
- your family background
- your hobbies (unless they're directly relevant)
- your primary school history
- unrelated personal experiences

Instead, they're asking for your **professional introduction**.

Think of this answer as the opening chapter of your interview.

It should encourage the interviewer to learn more—not overwhelm them with unnecessary detail.

For example, compare these two approaches:

| Weak Approach | Strong Approach |
| --- | --- |
| Starts with childhood and unrelated personal details. | Focuses immediately on education, experience and career goals. |
| Repeats the entire CV. | Highlights only the most relevant experiences. |
| Lasts four or five minutes. | Takes about one to two minutes. |
| Ends without connecting to the role. | Explains why the opportunity is appealing. |

A focused introduction demonstrates preparation, confidence and respect for the interviewer's time.

# The Perfect Four-Part Framework

One of the easiest ways to answer this question is by following a simple four-part structure.

Rather than memorising a script, use this framework to organise your thoughts.

We'll explore each stage in detail throughout this guide.

| Step | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| **Current Situation** | Explain where you are professionally or academically. |
| **Relevant Background** | Highlight education, experience or achievements related to the role. |
| **Strengths** | Mention the skills or qualities that make you a strong candidate. |
| **Why This Opportunity** | Connect your background to the position you're applying for. |

This structure keeps your answer focused while naturally leading into the rest of the interview.
# What to Include in Your Answer

A strong answer to **"Tell me about yourself"** should be relevant to the role you're applying for.

Think of your response as a short professional introduction rather than a biography. Every sentence should help the interviewer understand why you're a suitable candidate.

Most successful answers include four key elements.

## 1. Start with Your Current Situation

Begin by briefly explaining where you are in your career.

This gives the interviewer immediate context.

For example:

- "I'm a recent Finance graduate from Kenyatta University."
- "I'm currently completing my Bachelor's degree in Information Technology."
- "I've been working as a customer service representative for the past three years."

Keep this section brief.

The goal is simply to establish your current professional or academic position.

---

## 2. Highlight Relevant Experience

Next, explain the experiences that have prepared you for this opportunity.

If you're a student or recent graduate, you might discuss:

- university projects
- industrial attachment
- internships
- volunteering
- leadership positions
- student organisations
- research projects
- freelance work

If you already have professional experience, focus on roles and achievements that relate directly to the position.

Avoid listing every job you've ever held.

Instead, highlight the experiences that best demonstrate your suitability for this opportunity.

---

## 3. Mention Your Key Strengths

Recruiters also want to understand what you believe you bring to the role.

Choose two or three strengths that are supported by evidence.

Examples include:

- analytical thinking
- communication skills
- teamwork
- leadership
- organisation
- adaptability
- customer service
- attention to detail
- problem-solving

Rather than simply saying:

> "I'm hardworking."

Explain how you've demonstrated that quality.

For example:

> "During my final-year project, I coordinated a team of four students, organised weekly meetings and ensured we completed every milestone before the deadline."

Specific examples make your strengths more believable.

---

## 4. Connect Your Background to the Role

Finish by explaining why you're interested in the position.

This is the transition that naturally leads into the rest of the interview.

You might mention:

- your enthusiasm for the organisation
- your interest in the industry
- your career goals
- opportunities to learn
- how the role aligns with your long-term plans

For example:

> "I'm excited about this opportunity because it will allow me to apply the analytical skills I've developed at university while learning from experienced professionals in the financial sector."

Ending with the role reminds the interviewer why you're sitting in front of them.

---

# What to Leave Out

Knowing what **not** to say is just as important as knowing what to include.

Many candidates lose the interviewer's attention by adding information that doesn't support their application.

Avoid discussing:

- your childhood
- your family background
- your religion
- your political opinions
- unrelated hobbies
- personal challenges that don't relate to the role
- negative experiences with previous employers
- salary expectations during your introduction

Remember that this is a professional conversation.

Everything you say should help answer one question:

> **Why should this organisation consider hiring me?**

If a piece of information doesn't help answer that question, it's usually best to leave it out.

---

# How Long Should Your Answer Be?

One of the biggest mistakes candidates make is talking for too long.

A strong answer usually lasts between **60 and 90 seconds**.

For senior roles, you may naturally speak for up to two minutes if your experience is highly relevant.

Anything significantly longer risks losing the interviewer's attention.

A useful guideline is:

- Current position: 15–20 seconds
- Relevant experience: 25–35 seconds
- Strengths: 20–30 seconds
- Why this role: 15–20 seconds

This creates a balanced answer that is informative without feeling rushed.

Remember that interviews are conversations.

Your introduction should invite follow-up questions rather than answer everything at once.

---

# Should You Memorise Your Answer?

Many candidates worry about forgetting what they want to say.

As a result, they memorise an answer word for word.

Unfortunately, this often has the opposite effect.

Memorised answers can sound:

- robotic
- rehearsed
- unnatural
- disconnected from the conversation

Instead of memorising sentences, memorise your structure.

Know the four parts of your answer:

1. Current situation
2. Relevant background
3. Key strengths
4. Why this opportunity

This allows you to adapt naturally while still covering every important point.

If the interviewer interrupts with a follow-up question, you'll also be able to respond comfortably without losing your place.

> **Interview Tip**
>
> Practice your introduction until it feels like a conversation rather than a speech. Recording yourself or practising with a friend can help you identify areas where your delivery sounds too rehearsed or where you're including unnecessary detail.

# Tailoring Your Answer to Different Roles

No two interviews are exactly the same.

A strong introduction should always be adapted to the position you're applying for.

For example, an internship interview should emphasise your education and willingness to learn, while an experienced professional should focus on measurable achievements and industry expertise.

The following examples demonstrate how the same question can be answered effectively at different stages of your career.

We'll begin with the type of answer most students and recent graduates need for internship interviews.
# Sample Answers for Different Interview Situations

Every interview is different.

A university student applying for an internship should introduce themselves differently from someone with five years of professional experience.

The examples below are designed to show how the same framework can be adapted to different career stages while remaining authentic and relevant.

Don't memorise these answers word for word.

Instead, use them as inspiration to create an introduction that reflects your own education, experience and career goals.

---

# Example 1: Internship Interview

This example is suitable for university students, TVET graduates or candidates applying for industrial attachment and internship opportunities.

> **Sample Answer**
>
> "I'm currently in my final year pursuing a Bachelor's degree in Procurement and Supply Chain Management at Jomo Kenyatta University of Agriculture and Technology. Throughout my studies, I've developed a strong interest in logistics and inventory management, particularly through group projects where I worked closely with classmates to solve real business case studies.
>
> Last year, I completed an industrial attachment with a local manufacturing company, where I assisted the procurement team with supplier documentation, stock reconciliation and purchase order tracking. That experience strengthened both my communication and organisational skills while giving me practical exposure to a professional working environment.
>
> I'm now looking for an internship where I can continue learning, contribute to a collaborative team and build practical experience that complements my academic background. That's one of the reasons I was excited to apply for this opportunity."

### Why This Answer Works

The candidate:

- immediately establishes their current academic status
- highlights relevant practical experience
- demonstrates transferable skills
- finishes by connecting their goals to the internship

Even without years of work experience, the answer presents a clear professional identity.

---

# Example 2: Graduate Trainee Programme

Graduate trainee programmes typically recruit recent graduates with strong academic foundations and leadership potential.

Recruiters are looking for candidates who demonstrate curiosity, adaptability and a willingness to develop.

> **Sample Answer**
>
> "I recently graduated with a Bachelor's degree in Economics from the University of Nairobi. During my studies, I developed a strong interest in financial analysis and business strategy, and I actively participated in student leadership and case competition events that strengthened my analytical and teamwork skills.
>
> I also volunteered with a youth empowerment organisation, where I helped organise financial literacy workshops for university students. That experience improved my communication skills and reinforced my interest in working on projects that create measurable impact.
>
> I'm excited about this graduate trainee programme because it offers structured learning, mentorship and exposure across different business functions. I believe it's an excellent environment to develop professionally while contributing my enthusiasm and willingness to learn."

### Why This Answer Works

Notice that the candidate doesn't apologise for being a recent graduate.

Instead, they confidently connect:

- academic achievements
- extracurricular experience
- transferable skills
- career ambitions

The answer demonstrates potential rather than experience.

---

# Example 3: Entry-Level Professional

This example suits candidates with one to three years of experience.

> **Sample Answer**
>
> "I'm currently working as a Customer Service Officer, where I've spent the past two years helping clients resolve enquiries, processing customer requests and supporting the sales team. During that time, I've consistently exceeded our customer satisfaction targets while developing strong communication and problem-solving skills.
>
> What I've enjoyed most is building relationships with customers and finding practical solutions to their challenges. Those experiences have strengthened my ability to remain calm under pressure while working effectively within a team.
>
> I'm now looking for an opportunity where I can continue developing professionally while taking on greater responsibility. Your organisation's focus on customer experience and employee development is one of the reasons this role stood out to me."

### Why This Answer Works

The candidate:

- demonstrates measurable experience
- highlights achievements
- explains motivation for changing roles
- finishes by linking their goals to the employer

This creates a logical narrative rather than simply repeating the CV.

# Common Patterns You'll Notice

Although these examples represent different career stages, they all follow the same structure.

Each answer:

- starts with the candidate's current position
- highlights the most relevant experience
- demonstrates strengths through examples
- ends by connecting their background to the opportunity

This consistency is what makes the framework so effective.

As your career progresses, the details will change—but the overall structure remains largely the same.
# Example 4: Experienced Professional

Candidates with several years of experience should shift the focus from education to achievements, responsibilities and measurable results.

Recruiters already expect technical competence.

Your introduction should demonstrate the value you've created throughout your career.

> **Sample Answer**
>
> "I'm a Human Resources professional with over six years of experience supporting recruitment, employee relations and performance management within the manufacturing sector. Throughout my career, I've enjoyed building efficient recruitment processes and helping organisations attract and retain talented employees.
>
> In my current position, I've led several recruitment campaigns, introduced improvements to our onboarding process and worked closely with departmental managers to improve employee engagement. These initiatives have helped reduce recruitment turnaround times while improving the overall candidate experience.
>
> I'm now looking for an opportunity where I can contribute my experience in a larger organisation while continuing to grow professionally. Your organisation's reputation for investing in people and developing strong workplace cultures is one of the reasons I was excited to apply."

### Why This Answer Works

The candidate focuses on:

- years of relevant experience
- measurable contributions
- professional strengths
- future career goals
- genuine interest in the organisation

Notice that there is no unnecessary career history.

Only the most relevant information is included.

# Example 5: Government Job Interview

Government interviews often place significant emphasis on professionalism, accountability and public service.

Your introduction should demonstrate competence while reflecting an understanding of the organisation's mandate.

> **Sample Answer**
>
> "I'm a graduate of Public Administration with practical experience gained through my internship at a county government office, where I supported records management, public enquiries and administrative coordination.
>
> During that experience, I developed a strong appreciation for the importance of accountability, professionalism and delivering quality services to members of the public. I also strengthened my organisational and communication skills by working with different departments to ensure daily operations ran efficiently.
>
> I'm interested in joining the public service because I believe it offers an opportunity to contribute meaningfully to national development while continuing to develop my administrative and policy implementation skills."

### Why This Answer Works

Government recruiters often value candidates who demonstrate:

- integrity
- professionalism
- commitment to public service
- understanding of the institution's responsibilities

The answer reflects these values naturally.

# Example 6: NGO Interview

Many NGOs look beyond technical qualifications.

They also assess whether candidates genuinely understand and support the organisation's mission.

> **Sample Answer**
>
> "I'm a Social Sciences graduate with experience coordinating community outreach activities through university volunteer programmes and local youth initiatives. Working directly with different communities strengthened my communication, teamwork and project coordination skills while reinforcing my passion for community development.
>
> Over the past year, I've also volunteered with an organisation supporting youth employability, where I assisted with workshop planning and participant engagement. That experience taught me the importance of listening carefully to community needs and working collaboratively to develop practical solutions.
>
> I'm excited about this opportunity because your organisation's work aligns closely with the type of impact I hope to contribute throughout my career."

### Why This Answer Works

The candidate demonstrates:

- genuine alignment with the organisation's mission
- relevant practical experience
- transferable skills
- long-term motivation

Recruiters often find mission-driven answers more convincing than generic statements about wanting a job.

# Example 7: Banking Interview

Banks often look for professionalism, accuracy, analytical thinking and excellent customer service.

Your introduction should reflect those qualities.

> **Sample Answer**
>
> "I'm a Finance graduate with practical experience gained through my attachment in a commercial bank, where I supported customer onboarding, account documentation and daily operational tasks.
>
> During that period, I developed a strong appreciation for accuracy, confidentiality and delivering excellent customer service. I also strengthened my analytical skills through financial reporting projects at university and by using spreadsheet tools to analyse business data.
>
> I'm excited about this opportunity because it offers the chance to continue developing within the financial services industry while contributing to an organisation known for professionalism and innovation."

### Why This Answer Works

The answer highlights qualities commonly assessed in banking interviews, including:

- attention to detail
- trustworthiness
- professionalism
- customer focus
- analytical ability

# Example 8: Career Change

Changing careers is becoming increasingly common.

The key is to explain your transition positively.

Avoid criticising your previous industry.

Instead, focus on the transferable skills you've developed.

> **Sample Answer**
>
> "For the past five years, I've worked in retail operations, where I developed strong customer service, problem-solving and team leadership skills. Managing daily operations taught me how to work under pressure, resolve customer concerns and coordinate teams to achieve performance targets.
>
> Over time, I became increasingly interested in digital marketing and began completing online courses while managing social media campaigns for a small family business. Through those projects, I discovered how much I enjoy analysing campaign performance and creating content that connects with customers.
>
> I'm now looking for an opportunity to transition into digital marketing full-time, where I can combine my customer-facing experience with the technical skills I've been developing over the past year."

### Why This Answer Works

Successful career-change answers explain:

- where you've been
- what you've learned
- why you're changing direction
- how your existing skills remain valuable

This reassures recruiters that your transition is thoughtful rather than impulsive.

# Adapting These Examples to Your Own Situation

No sample answer can perfectly describe your background.

Your goal isn't to copy these examples.

It's to understand the structure behind them.

As you prepare your own introduction, ask yourself:

- What experiences best demonstrate my potential?
- Which achievements are most relevant to this role?
- What strengths can I support with real examples?
- Why am I genuinely interested in this opportunity?

Answering these questions will help you create an introduction that feels authentic rather than rehearsed.

Remember that interviewers appreciate honesty and clarity far more than perfectly memorised speeches.
# Delivering Your Answer with Confidence

Having a well-structured answer is important, but **how you deliver it** also influences the interviewer's first impression.

Even an excellent answer can lose its impact if it's delivered too quickly, too quietly or without confidence.

The good news is that confident delivery is a skill you can practise.

## Speak at a Natural Pace

When people become nervous, they often begin speaking much faster than usual.

This can make your introduction difficult to follow.

Instead:

- pause before answering
- take a comfortable breath
- speak slightly slower than you would during a casual conversation
- pause briefly between ideas

A measured pace communicates confidence and gives the interviewer time to absorb what you're saying.

---

## Maintain Professional Eye Contact

If your interview is in person, maintain comfortable eye contact throughout your introduction.

You don't need to stare continuously.

Instead, aim for natural eye contact while occasionally looking away as you think.

If your interview is virtual, look at your camera regularly rather than only watching yourself on the screen.

This creates the impression that you're speaking directly to the interviewer.

---

## Smile Naturally

A genuine smile helps create a positive first impression.

You don't need to smile throughout your answer.

Simply begin warmly and allow your facial expressions to reflect genuine enthusiasm for the opportunity.

---

## Avoid Filler Words

Many candidates unintentionally fill pauses with words such as:

- "um"
- "uh"
- "you know"
- "like"
- "basically"

Using an occasional filler word is normal.

However, frequent fillers can distract from an otherwise strong introduction.

If you lose your train of thought, pause briefly instead of filling the silence.

A short pause appears far more confident than unnecessary filler words.

---

## Let Your Personality Show

Interviewers are assessing whether you'd be someone they'd enjoy working with.

Don't try to sound like someone else.

Speak professionally, but allow your enthusiasm and personality to come through naturally.

Authenticity is far more memorable than a perfectly rehearsed performance.

> **Interview Tip**
>
> Confidence doesn't mean pretending to know everything. It means communicating clearly, honestly and professionally while demonstrating that you're ready to learn.

# Common Follow-Up Questions

Your introduction often determines the direction of the interview.

Interviewers frequently ask follow-up questions based on something you've mentioned.

For example, if you mention leading a university project, they may ask:

- "Can you tell me more about that project?"
- "What challenges did your team face?"
- "What was your specific role?"

If you discuss an internship, they might ask:

- "What were your daily responsibilities?"
- "What did you learn from the experience?"
- "How has that experience prepared you for this role?"

If you mention leadership experience, expect questions such as:

- "Describe a time you resolved conflict within your team."
- "How do you motivate others?"
- "Tell me about a difficult decision you had to make."

Many of these are behavioural interview questions.

If you'd like to prepare structured answers, read our guide on **[Behavioural Interview Questions in Kenya (2026): 30 Examples with Expert Answers](/resources/interview-preparation/behavioural-interview-questions-kenya-2026)**.

# 20 Common Mistakes to Avoid

Strong candidates don't just know what to say.

They also know what to avoid.

## 1. Telling Your Entire Life Story

Your answer should focus on your professional background, not your personal history.

---

## 2. Reading Your CV Word for Word

The interviewer has already reviewed your CV.

Use your introduction to provide context, not repetition.

---

## 3. Speaking for Too Long

Aim for one to two minutes.

A concise answer is easier to follow and leaves room for conversation.

---

## 4. Giving a Memorised Speech

A rehearsed structure is helpful.

A memorised script often sounds unnatural.

---

## 5. Providing Generic Statements

Avoid unsupported claims such as:

- "I'm hardworking."
- "I'm a team player."
- "I'm passionate."

Support every important quality with a relevant example.

---

## 6. Speaking Negatively About Previous Employers

Even if you've had difficult experiences, keep your introduction positive.

Professionalism matters.

---

## 7. Including Irrelevant Personal Information

Details about your childhood, family or unrelated hobbies rarely strengthen your answer.

Focus on information that supports your application.

---

## 8. Forgetting to Connect Your Background to the Role

Your answer should always explain why you're interested in this specific opportunity.

Don't leave the interviewer wondering how your background relates to the position.

---

## 9. Using Too Much Industry Jargon

Explain your experience clearly.

Avoid assuming the interviewer is familiar with technical terminology from your previous workplace.

---

## 10. Sounding Uninterested

Your words and tone should reflect genuine enthusiasm.

Recruiters often remember candidates who show authentic interest in the organisation.

---

## 11. Exaggerating Your Experience

Be honest about your achievements.

If the interviewer asks follow-up questions, exaggerated claims quickly become obvious.

---

## 12. Forgetting Important Achievements

Review your CV before the interview.

You should be able to discuss every major project, internship and responsibility you've listed.

---

## 13. Speaking Too Quietly

Make sure the interviewer can hear you comfortably.

Confidence is difficult to demonstrate if your answers aren't audible.

---

## 14. Rushing Because You're Nervous

A brief pause before answering is perfectly acceptable.

Speaking more slowly often makes you appear more confident.

---

## 15. Ignoring Your Body Language

Maintain good posture, avoid excessive fidgeting and use natural hand gestures where appropriate.

Your body language should reinforce your words.

---

## 16. Failing to Research the Organisation

Your final sentence should demonstrate that you've thought carefully about why you're applying.

Research makes this much easier.

---

## 17. Trying to Sound Perfect

Interviewers don't expect perfection.

They appreciate honesty, preparation and self-awareness.

---

## 18. Giving Different Answers to Different Interviewers

If you're interviewed by a panel, ensure your introduction remains consistent throughout the conversation.

---

## 19. Not Practising Aloud

Reading your answer silently isn't enough.

Practice speaking it aloud until it feels comfortable and conversational.

---

## 20. Forgetting That This Is Only the Beginning

Your introduction isn't intended to answer every interview question.

Its purpose is to establish credibility and create a strong first impression before the interview moves into more detailed discussions.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## Should I Memorise My Entire Answer?

No.

Instead of memorising every sentence, memorise the structure of your answer.

This allows you to sound natural while ensuring you cover your most important points.

Interviewers generally prefer candidates who communicate authentically rather than those who deliver rehearsed speeches.

---

## What If I Have No Work Experience?

This is common for internship and graduate interviews.

Focus on experiences such as:

- university projects
- industrial attachment
- volunteering
- student leadership
- freelance work
- extracurricular activities

Recruiters understand that students may not have extensive employment history.

They're interested in your potential, willingness to learn and transferable skills.

---

## How Long Should My Answer Be?

Aim for **60 to 90 seconds**.

If you have significant professional experience, your answer may naturally extend to around two minutes.

Anything much longer can make it difficult for the interviewer to remain engaged.

---

## Should I Talk About My Personal Life?

Only if it's directly relevant to the position.

In most interviews, your introduction should focus on your professional background, education, experience and career goals.

Personal details should only be included when they help explain your motivation or qualifications.

---

## What If I Become Nervous During My Introduction?

Nervousness is completely normal.

If you lose your train of thought:

- pause briefly
- take a breath
- continue calmly

A short pause appears far more professional than rushing through your answer or apologising repeatedly.

Remember that interviewers expect candidates to feel some level of nervousness.

---

## Can I Mention Hobbies?

Yes—but only if they're relevant.

For example:

- building software projects
- volunteering
- writing professional articles
- participating in debate competitions
- leading community initiatives

Avoid spending valuable time discussing hobbies that don't strengthen your application.

---

## Is It Acceptable to Mention Career Goals?

Absolutely.

In fact, ending your answer by explaining how the role fits into your career plans demonstrates motivation and long-term thinking.

Keep your goals realistic and aligned with the opportunity you're applying for.

---

## How Can I Practise Effectively?

One of the best methods is to record yourself answering the question.

Afterwards, listen carefully and ask yourself:

- Did I stay within two minutes?
- Was my answer well organised?
- Did I sound confident?
- Did I provide evidence for my strengths?
- Did I explain why I wanted the role?

Practising with a friend, mentor or careers adviser can also provide valuable feedback.

---

- □ highlights your most relevant experience
- □ demonstrates two or three strengths using real examples
- □ explains why you're interested in the role
- □ lasts around one to two minutes
- □ sounds natural rather than memorised
- □ avoids unnecessary personal details
- □ ends confidently and positively

If you can confidently tick every item, you're well prepared to answer one of the most important questions in almost any interview.

# Key Takeaways

The question **"Tell me about yourself"** isn't intended to test your memory or encourage you to recite your CV.

It's an opportunity to introduce yourself professionally and shape the direction of the interview.

The strongest answers:

- focus on relevant experience rather than personal history
- follow a clear structure
- support strengths with evidence
- explain genuine interest in the opportunity
- remain concise and conversational

With preparation and practice, this question can become one of the easiest—and most effective—parts of your interview.

Rather than fearing it, treat it as your opportunity to make an excellent first impression before the more detailed interview questions begin.
