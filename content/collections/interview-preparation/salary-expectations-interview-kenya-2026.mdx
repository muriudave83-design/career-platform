# How to Answer "What Are Your Salary Expectations?" in an Interview (Kenya 2026)

Sooner or later, almost every interview reaches a question that makes candidates pause:

> **"What are your salary expectations?"**

Many people worry that saying a number that's too high will cost them the job.

Others fear that asking for too little will leave them underpaid if they receive an offer.

It's a difficult balance, especially for internship applicants, recent graduates and professionals changing careers.

The good news is that this question isn't designed to catch you out.

In most cases, recruiters ask about salary expectations because they want to understand whether your expectations are broadly aligned with the role and the organisation's budget.

A thoughtful answer demonstrates preparation, professionalism and an understanding of your own market value.

Throughout this guide, you'll learn how to answer salary questions confidently using the **JoinNexiva VALUE™ Framework**.

You'll discover:

- why employers ask about salary
- how to research realistic salary ranges
- when to give a range instead of a fixed figure
- how to respond if you don't know the market rate
- sample answers for internships, graduate programmes and experienced professionals
- negotiation tips
- common mistakes to avoid
- Kenya-specific interview scenarios

Rather than memorising a single response, you'll learn how to adapt your answer to different situations while remaining professional and flexible.

# Quick Answer

When an interviewer asks about your salary expectations:

- research the role before the interview
- understand the typical market range
- communicate a realistic expectation
- explain that you're flexible where appropriate
- focus on finding a role that's a good mutual fit

Avoid guessing or naming a figure without preparation.

Instead, use the **JoinNexiva VALUE™ Framework** to structure a confident and informed response.

# Why Recruiters Ask This Question

Many candidates believe this question is simply about negotiating pay.

In reality, recruiters often have several objectives.

They want to understand whether:

- your expectations align with the approved budget
- you've researched the role
- you're realistic about the market
- compensation is likely to become a barrier later in the hiring process
- you're approaching the discussion professionally

Salary is only one part of the conversation.

Recruiters are also assessing your judgement, communication skills and preparation.

> **JoinNexiva Recruiter Insight**
>
> Strong candidates don't rush to state a number. They demonstrate that they've researched the role, understand the market and are open to a fair discussion based on the responsibilities of the position.

# What Recruiters Are Really Assessing

When recruiters ask about salary expectations, they're rarely looking for a single "correct" number.

Instead, they're asking questions such as:

- Have you researched the market?
- Do you understand the level of this role?
- Are your expectations realistic?
- Can we continue the hiring process without major misalignment?
- Will you approach compensation discussions professionally?

Understanding this helps reduce unnecessary anxiety.

The conversation is usually about alignment—not trying to trick you into accepting less than you're worth.
# Step 1: Verify the Role Before Discussing Salary

The first step of the **JoinNexiva VALUE™ Framework** is understanding exactly what you're being paid to do.

Salary should never be discussed in isolation.

Instead, consider:

- the responsibilities of the role
- required qualifications
- level of experience
- location
- working hours
- contract type
- additional benefits

Two jobs with the same title can have very different responsibilities and compensation.

Before deciding on a salary expectation, make sure you understand what the employer is actually offering.

> **JoinNexiva Recruiter Insight**
>
> Candidates who ask thoughtful questions about the role before discussing salary often appear more professional than those who immediately focus on compensation.

---

# Step 2: Assess the Value You Bring

Next, evaluate your own profile honestly.

Ask yourself:

- What education do I have?
- What practical experience have I gained?
- Do I have internships or attachments?
- What technical skills do I possess?
- Which soft skills make me effective?
- Do I hold relevant certifications?

Your salary expectations should reflect both the market and the value you bring.

For example:

### Internship Candidate

May primarily offer:

- willingness to learn
- academic knowledge
- enthusiasm
- adaptability

---

### Graduate Candidate

May additionally offer:

- internship experience
- research projects
- leadership experience
- certifications
- technical skills

---

### Experienced Professional

May contribute:

- measurable business results
- leadership
- specialist expertise
- client relationships
- industry knowledge

The stronger your evidence of value, the stronger your position during salary discussions.

# Step 3: Learn the Market

One of the biggest mistakes candidates make is walking into an interview without researching typical salaries.

Research helps you understand:

- common salary ranges
- industry standards
- regional differences
- experience levels
- employer expectations

Don't rely on guesses or rumours.

Instead, combine multiple sources such as:

- job advertisements (where salaries are published)
- industry salary surveys
- professional networks
- conversations with trusted mentors
- publicly available compensation reports

Your goal isn't to find one exact figure.

It's to understand a reasonable range.

---

# Should You Give a Range or One Number?

In many situations, providing a **reasonable salary range** is more effective than stating one fixed amount.

For example:

> "Based on my research, similar graduate roles typically fall within this range. Considering my education and internship experience, I believe a salary within that range would be reasonable, although I'm open to discussing the overall package."

A range demonstrates:

- preparation
- flexibility
- professionalism

Avoid making the range excessively wide.

It should still communicate realistic expectations.

---

# Step 4: Understand the Employer's Perspective

Salary discussions aren't only about you.

Employers also have constraints.

These may include:

- approved budgets
- internal salary structures
- fairness across employees
- market competitiveness
- benefits packages

Understanding this changes the tone of the conversation.

Instead of negotiating against the employer, you're working towards an agreement that suits both sides.

This collaborative mindset often leads to more productive discussions.

---

# Step 5: Express Your Expectations Professionally

Once you've researched the role and assessed your value, communicate your expectations clearly.

Avoid apologising for discussing salary.

Likewise, avoid making demands.

Instead, aim for a balanced response.

For example:

> "Based on the responsibilities of the role, my qualifications and the research I've done on similar positions, I believe a salary within the typical market range would be appropriate. That said, I'm open to discussing the complete compensation package and learning more about the opportunities available within your organisation."

Notice the tone.

It's:

- respectful
- informed
- flexible
- confident

Those qualities matter just as much as the number itself.

# Salary Expectations by Career Stage

Your answer should reflect where you are in your career.

## Internship Applicants

Focus on:

- learning opportunities
- mentorship
- gaining practical experience
- fair compensation

Example:

> "My priority is gaining meaningful experience and contributing to your team. I understand internship programmes often have structured compensation, and I'm comfortable discussing a package that's consistent with the responsibilities of the role."

---

## Graduate Trainees

Graduate programmes usually balance learning with structured career development.

Example:

> "Based on my research into graduate programmes and my internship experience, I have a realistic expectation of the market. I'm primarily looking for an opportunity where I can continue developing while making meaningful contributions, and I'm happy to discuss a compensation package that reflects both."

---

## Experienced Professionals

Experienced candidates should connect compensation to demonstrated results.

Example:

> "Given my experience leading similar projects, improving operational performance and delivering measurable outcomes, I'm looking for compensation that reflects the value I can bring. At the same time, I'm open to discussing the overall package and learning more about the responsibilities of this position."

# Kenya-Specific Salary Discussion Scenarios

Every industry approaches compensation differently.

Understanding the context helps you respond professionally without making assumptions.

---

## Graduate Programme

Many graduate programmes have structured salary bands.

Example:

> **Interviewer:** "What are your salary expectations?"
>
> **Candidate:**
>
> "I understand that graduate programmes often have established salary structures. Based on my research into similar programmes and my internship experience, I'm looking for a competitive package that's aligned with the responsibilities of the role. I'm happy to discuss the complete compensation package."

### Why This Works

The candidate:

- recognises structured pay
- demonstrates research
- remains flexible
- avoids quoting an unrealistic figure

---

## Banking Sector

Banks often value professionalism and preparation.

Example:

> "From my research into similar banking roles and considering my previous customer service experience, I have a realistic expectation of the market. I'm confident we can agree on a package that reflects both the role's responsibilities and the value I hope to contribute."

---

## ICT and Technology

Technology salaries can vary significantly depending on skills and experience.

Example:

> "Because technical roles vary considerably depending on responsibilities and technologies used, I'd like to understand more about the position before discussing a specific figure. Based on my research, I have a reasonable range in mind and would be happy to discuss it."

---

## Government

Government positions usually follow established salary grades.

Example:

> "I understand this position follows a structured compensation framework. My primary interest is contributing effectively to the organisation, and I'm comfortable discussing compensation within the approved structure for the role."

---

## NGO

Many NGOs balance salary with purpose-driven work and broader benefits.

Example:

> "I'm looking for a fair package that's consistent with the responsibilities of the role and the sector. I'm also interested in understanding the broader benefits, professional development opportunities and the overall package."

# Negotiation Dos and Don'ts

Salary discussions don't begin after you receive an offer.

They begin with your professionalism throughout the recruitment process.

## Do

- Research before every interview.
- Understand typical market ranges.
- Listen carefully before responding.
- Remain respectful throughout the discussion.
- Consider the entire compensation package.
- Be prepared to explain your reasoning.
- Stay flexible where appropriate.

---

## Don't

- Guess a figure.
- Demand a salary without justification.
- Compare yourself negatively with other candidates.
- Become defensive if the employer proposes a different figure.
- Accept an offer immediately without understanding the full package if you need time to consider it.
- Focus only on salary while ignoring benefits, learning opportunities and career progression.

# 15 Common Salary Discussion Mistakes

Avoid these common pitfalls.

## 1. Walking Into the Interview Without Research

Preparation builds confidence.

---

## 2. Giving the First Number Without Thinking

Pause.

Consider the role before responding.

---

## 3. Choosing a Number Based Only on Personal Expenses

Employers pay for the value required by the role—not your monthly budget.

---

## 4. Undervaluing Yourself

Many candidates quote figures far below the market because they're afraid of losing the opportunity.

---

## 5. Overpricing Yourself Without Evidence

High expectations should be supported by relevant experience and skills.

---

## 6. Refusing to Discuss Salary

Avoid appearing uncomfortable with professional conversations.

---

## 7. Appearing Interested Only in Money

Balance compensation discussions with enthusiasm for the role itself.

---

## 8. Ignoring Benefits

Salary is only one part of the package.

Consider:

- medical cover
- pension
- bonuses
- training
- flexible working arrangements
- career progression

---

## 9. Speaking Emotionally

Keep the conversation professional.

---

## 10. Comparing Employers

Avoid saying:

> "Company X pays more."

Focus on the opportunity in front of you.

---

## 11. Being Inflexible

Negotiation requires discussion—not ultimatums.

---

## 12. Forgetting Your Achievements

Support your expectations with evidence.

---

## 13. Accepting Immediately Under Pressure

If appropriate, ask for time to review the complete offer.

---

## 14. Negotiating Before Understanding the Role

Clarify responsibilities first.

---

## 15. Ending the Conversation Negatively

Always remain courteous, even if you don't agree with the initial offer.

Professionalism leaves a lasting impression.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## Should I Give an Exact Figure or a Range?

A realistic range is often more appropriate because it demonstrates preparation while leaving room for discussion.

---

## What If I Don't Know the Market Salary?

Research before the interview whenever possible.

If you're genuinely unsure, explain that you'd like to learn more about the role and the organisation's compensation structure before discussing specific expectations.

---

## Is It Okay to Say I'm Flexible?

Yes.

Flexibility demonstrates professionalism, provided you've also shown that you've researched the role.

---

## Can I Negotiate After Receiving an Offer?

Yes.

Many employers expect reasonable negotiation.

Approach the conversation respectfully, support your request with evidence and remain open to discussion.

---

## Should Salary Be My Main Reason for Taking a Job?

Compensation matters, but it shouldn't be the only consideration.

Think about:

- learning opportunities
- career progression
- organisational culture
- mentorship
- long-term growth

The best opportunities often provide value beyond your monthly salary.
