# How to Answer "What Are Your Strengths and Weaknesses?" in an Interview (Kenya 2026)

Few interview questions reveal more about a candidate than:

> **"What are your strengths?"**

and

> **"What is your greatest weakness?"**

Almost every recruiter asks some variation of these questions, whether you're interviewing for an internship, a graduate trainee programme or an experienced professional role.

For many candidates, they're also among the most uncomfortable questions to answer.

Talk too confidently about your strengths and you risk sounding arrogant.

Choose the wrong weakness and you may unintentionally damage your chances of getting the job.

As a result, many candidates rely on overused responses like:

- "I'm a perfectionist."
- "I work too hard."
- "I care too much."

Recruiters have heard these answers countless times.

They rarely provide meaningful insight into how you'll perform in the workplace.

The good news is that there is a better approach.

Instead of trying to invent the "perfect" answer, you should focus on demonstrating self-awareness, credibility and a willingness to grow.

That's exactly what this guide will help you do.

You'll learn:

- why recruiters ask about strengths and weaknesses
- what they're really assessing
- how to choose strengths that support your application
- how to discuss weaknesses honestly without undermining your credibility
- practical examples for internships, graduate programmes and experienced professionals
- common mistakes that cause otherwise strong candidates to struggle

Along the way, we'll also introduce the **JoinNexiva CLEAR™ Method**—a practical framework for answering these questions confidently while remaining authentic.

# Quick Answer

Recruiters ask about your strengths and weaknesses to understand how well you know yourself.

They're looking for candidates who can:

- identify genuine strengths supported by evidence
- acknowledge areas for improvement honestly
- demonstrate a willingness to learn and develop
- communicate with confidence and professionalism

The strongest answers don't rely on clichés.

Instead, they provide specific examples that show how your strengths have helped you succeed and how you're actively improving your weaknesses.

---

# Why Recruiters Ask This Question

At first glance, this question seems straightforward.

In reality, recruiters are assessing much more than the words you choose.

They're interested in your level of self-awareness.

Can you evaluate your own performance honestly?

Do you understand what you do well?

Can you recognise areas where you still have room to improve?

These qualities matter in every workplace.

Employees who seek feedback, learn from mistakes and continuously improve often develop faster than those who believe they have nothing left to learn.

> **Recruiter's Perspective**
>
> During interviews, we don't expect candidates to be perfect. We look for people who understand both their strengths and their development areas—and who can explain what they're doing to improve.

---

# What Recruiters Are Really Assessing

Behind every answer, interviewers are trying to answer five important questions.

| What They Hear | What They're Really Assessing |
| --- | --- |
| Your strengths | Can you contribute value immediately? |
| Your weaknesses | Are you self-aware and coachable? |
| Your examples | Are your claims believable? |
| Your communication | Can you organise your thoughts clearly? |
| Your attitude | Will you continue learning after you're hired? |

Understanding these hidden objectives makes it much easier to answer the question effectively.

Rather than trying to impress the interviewer with exaggerated strengths or "safe" weaknesses, your goal is to demonstrate honesty, professionalism and growth.

# The JoinNexiva CLEAR™ Method

One mistake candidates often make is treating strengths and weaknesses as two completely separate questions.

In reality, recruiters are trying to build a complete picture of how you work.

To answer effectively, use the **JoinNexiva CLEAR™ Method**:

| Step | Meaning | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **C** | **Choose** | Select strengths and weaknesses that are genuinely relevant to the role. |
| **L** | **Link** | Support every strength with a real example or achievement. |
| **E** | **Explain** | Describe why the strength has helped you succeed—or how the weakness has affected you. |
| **A** | **Act** | Show the practical steps you're taking to improve your weakness. |
| **R** | **Relate** | Connect your answer back to the role you're applying for. |

The CLEAR™ Method prevents two common interview mistakes:

- making unsupported claims about your strengths
- mentioning a weakness without demonstrating growth

Instead, it helps you present yourself as someone who is both capable and committed to continuous improvement.

Throughout this guide, every sample answer will follow this framework.
# How to Choose the Right Strengths

One of the biggest mistakes candidates make is choosing strengths they think recruiters want to hear.

Instead of asking:

> "What sounds impressive?"

Ask:

> "Which strengths are most relevant to this role, and can I prove them with real examples?"

The second question leads to much stronger answers.

Remember that every strength you mention should satisfy two conditions:

1. It is relevant to the position.
2. You can support it with evidence.

If you can't demonstrate a strength through your education, work experience or achievements, it's better to choose another one.

## Match Your Strengths to the Job Description

Before your interview, carefully review the job advertisement.

Look for repeated words or requirements.

For example, if the employer repeatedly mentions:

- teamwork
- communication
- attention to detail
- customer service
- analytical thinking

those qualities are likely to be important for success in the role.

Your introduction should naturally highlight strengths that align with these requirements.

> **JoinNexiva Recruiter Insight**
>
> Recruiters are far more likely to remember candidates who demonstrate relevant strengths than candidates who simply list impressive-sounding qualities. Relevance always beats quantity.

# The Difference Between Claiming and Proving

Many candidates say:

> "I'm a strong leader."

That's a claim.

A stronger answer would be:

> "During my final year at university, I led a team of six students on our capstone project. We completed the project two weeks ahead of schedule and received one of the highest grades in the class. That experience strengthened my leadership, planning and communication skills."

The second answer is more convincing because it provides context and evidence.

Whenever possible, support your strengths using specific examples.

If you've prepared for behavioural interview questions, you'll recognise this approach from the **STAR Interview Method**, where real situations demonstrate your capabilities more effectively than broad statements.

# How to Talk About Your Weaknesses Honestly

For many candidates, discussing weaknesses feels like a trap.

They worry that admitting a weakness will immediately reduce their chances of getting the job.

In reality, recruiters already know that every candidate has weaknesses.

What they're trying to understand is:

- whether you're self-aware
- whether you're honest
- whether you're willing to improve
- whether your weakness is likely to affect your performance in the role

The strongest candidates don't pretend to be perfect.

They demonstrate maturity by acknowledging areas for development and explaining the practical steps they're taking to improve.

This is where the **JoinNexiva CLEAR™ Method** becomes especially valuable.

Rather than stopping after identifying a weakness, you continue the conversation by showing growth.

---

# What Makes a Good Interview Weakness?

A professional interview weakness should have four characteristics.

It should be:

- genuine
- improvable
- not essential to the role
- supported by evidence of improvement

For example:

> "Earlier in my university studies, I found public speaking challenging because I wasn't comfortable presenting in front of large groups. To improve, I volunteered to present more often during group projects and joined my department's debate club. While I still prepare carefully before presentations, I'm now much more confident speaking in front of different audiences."

This answer works because it demonstrates progress.

The interviewer finishes the answer believing you'll continue improving after you're hired.

---

# Weaknesses You Should Never Mention

Not every weakness is appropriate in an interview.

Avoid mentioning weaknesses that directly affect the essential responsibilities of the role.

For example:

| If You're Applying For... | Avoid Saying... |
| --- | --- |
| Accountant | "I'm not very good with numbers." |
| Customer Service | "I don't enjoy talking to people." |
| Driver | "I struggle to concentrate for long periods." |
| Software Developer | "I dislike learning new technology." |
| Sales Representative | "I'm uncomfortable speaking with strangers." |

Similarly, avoid weaknesses that suggest poor professionalism, such as:

- arriving late
- missing deadlines
- refusing feedback
- poor honesty
- lack of integrity
- disrespect for colleagues
- poor attendance

These are not development areas.

They're red flags.

---

# Professional Weaknesses That Can Be Discussed Effectively

Many development areas can become strong interview answers when presented honestly.

Examples include:

| Development Area | How to Show Improvement |
| --- | --- |
| Public speaking | Volunteering for presentations and practising communication. |
| Delegation | Learning to trust team members and distribute responsibilities more effectively. |
| Asking for help | Becoming more comfortable seeking guidance earlier in projects. |
| Technical knowledge | Completing courses and certifications to strengthen skills. |
| Time estimation | Using planning tools and prioritisation techniques. |
| Confidence | Gaining experience through presentations, leadership and workplace responsibilities. |
| Perfectionism | Learning when work is ready for delivery instead of endlessly refining it. |
| Saying no | Improving workload management by setting realistic priorities. |

Notice that every example includes a clear improvement strategy.

That's what recruiters want to hear.

---

# The Difference Between Excuses and Growth

Many candidates accidentally turn their weakness into an excuse.

For example:

> "I'm usually late because Nairobi traffic is unpredictable."

This shifts responsibility to external circumstances.

A stronger answer would be:

> "Earlier in my career, I underestimated travel time for meetings. To address this, I now plan my routes in advance and leave earlier than necessary. That habit has helped me become much more reliable."

The difference is significant.

The first candidate blames circumstances.

The second candidate accepts responsibility and demonstrates improvement.

Recruiters consistently respond more positively to candidates who take ownership of their development.

# Sample Weakness Answers

The following examples demonstrate how to answer weakness questions professionally using the **JoinNexiva CLEAR™ Method**.

## Example 1: Internship Candidate

> **Interviewer:** "What's one area you're currently working to improve?"
>
> **Candidate:**
>
> "Earlier in university, I wasn't very confident speaking during group presentations. I realised that avoiding presentations wasn't helping me improve, so I began volunteering to present our team's work whenever possible. I also joined a student leadership programme where I regularly spoke during meetings. While I still prepare carefully before presenting, I'm much more confident than I was a year ago, and I'm continuing to improve."

### Why This Answer Works

Using the CLEAR™ Method:

- **Choose:** Public speaking.
- **Link:** University presentations.
- **Explain:** Recognises the challenge honestly.
- **Act:** Volunteered for more presentations and leadership opportunities.
- **Relate:** Demonstrates continuous improvement—an attractive quality in interns.

---

## Example 2: Graduate Trainee

> **Candidate:**
>
> "One area I've been improving is delegation. During university group projects, I often took on too much responsibility because I wanted to ensure everything met a high standard. Over time, I realised that trusting other team members and coordinating responsibilities more effectively produced better results. I've been intentionally practising delegation during volunteer projects, and it's helped me become a better team member."

### Why This Answer Works

The candidate avoids pretending the weakness is actually a strength.

Instead, they acknowledge a genuine development area while showing practical improvement.

---

## Example 3: Experienced Professional

> **Candidate:**
>
> "Earlier in my career, I found it difficult to say no when colleagues asked for help. While I wanted to be supportive, I occasionally accepted too many responsibilities at once. Over the past two years, I've improved my workload planning by prioritising tasks, communicating timelines clearly and discussing competing priorities with my manager. That has helped me support my colleagues while consistently meeting my own deadlines."

### Why This Answer Works

This answer demonstrates:

- self-awareness
- accountability
- professional growth
- improved time management

The interviewer sees someone who learns from experience rather than repeating the same mistakes.

# JoinNexiva Recruiter Insight

Many candidates spend so much time trying to choose the "perfect" weakness that they forget what recruiters actually value.

Recruiters rarely reject candidates because they admit a reasonable development area.

They are far more concerned about candidates who:

- appear dishonest
- avoid responsibility
- refuse feedback
- believe they have nothing left to learn

A thoughtful answer that demonstrates growth will almost always outperform a rehearsed answer that sounds insincere.

# Kenya-Specific Interview Example

Imagine you're interviewing for a Graduate Trainee position at a leading Kenyan bank such as KCB, Equity Bank, Co-operative Bank or NCBA.

A recruiter asks:

> **"Tell us about one weakness you've been working on."**

An effective response might be:

# 15 Mistakes That Can Weaken Your Answer

Even well-qualified candidates sometimes undermine themselves by approaching this question poorly.

Avoid these common mistakes.

## 1. Choosing Strengths Without Evidence

Anyone can claim to be hardworking.

Recruiters are persuaded by examples, not adjectives.

---

## 2. Turning Every Weakness into a Strength

Answers such as:

> "My weakness is that I work too hard."

rarely sound genuine.

Choose a real development area instead.

---

## 3. Giving Different Answers Throughout the Interview

Your strengths should remain consistent with the examples you share later.

Consistency builds credibility.

---

## 4. Using Generic Buzzwords

Avoid relying solely on words like:

- passionate
- dynamic
- innovative
- motivated

Explain what those qualities look like in practice.

---

## 5. Speaking Negatively About Yourself

Acknowledging a weakness doesn't mean criticising yourself.

Be balanced and professional.

---

## 6. Mentioning Critical Job Requirements as Weaknesses

If the role depends heavily on communication, don't say communication is your greatest weakness.

---

## 7. Over-Explaining

Answer the question directly.

Avoid long stories that lose focus.

---

## 8. Sounding Defensive

Accept your development areas confidently.

Recruiters respect accountability.

---

## 9. Forgetting the Improvement Step

Always explain what you're doing to improve.

Growth is often more important than the weakness itself.

---

## 10. Memorising Answers Word for Word

Frameworks create confidence.

Scripts often sound artificial.

---

## 11. Ignoring the Job Description

Your strengths should reflect the employer's priorities.

---

## 12. Giving Multiple Weaknesses

One well-explained weakness is enough.

---

## 13. Failing to Connect Your Strengths to Results

Explain how your strengths have benefited your team, project or organisation.

---

## 14. Exaggerating Your Abilities

Authenticity is always more convincing than perfection.

---

## 15. Forgetting That Interviews Are Conversations

Your answer should open the door to discussion—not end it.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## How Many Strengths Should I Mention?

One or two well-supported strengths are usually enough.

Quality is more persuasive than quantity.

---

## Can I Use Different Strengths for Different Interviews?

Yes.

In fact, you should tailor your strengths to each role, provided they're genuine and supported by evidence.

---

## Is "Perfectionism" Still a Good Weakness?

Not usually.

Recruiters hear this answer frequently, and it often sounds rehearsed.

Choose a genuine development area instead.

---

## What If I Can't Think of a Weakness?

Ask yourself:

- What skill have I deliberately worked to improve over the past two years?
- What feedback have lecturers, supervisors or managers given me?
- What challenge pushed me outside my comfort zone?

Those questions often reveal authentic development areas.

---

## Can Students Use University Examples?

Absolutely.

Recruiters understand that internship and graduate candidates may not have extensive work experience.

University projects, leadership positions, volunteering and industrial attachments provide excellent examples.
