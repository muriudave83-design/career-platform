# Graduate Presentation Exercises in Kenya (2026): The Complete Success Guide

Standing in front of a panel of recruiters and delivering a presentation is one of the most intimidating experiences many graduates face during recruitment.

Unlike traditional interviews, presentation exercises place candidates in situations where they must analyse information, organise their thoughts and communicate recommendations clearly—all while being observed by assessors.

For graduates who have never presented in a professional environment, the experience can feel overwhelming.

Questions immediately begin racing through your mind.

*"What if I forget what I wanted to say?"*

*"How much detail should I include?"*

*"What if the recruiters interrupt me?"*

*"Should I memorise everything?"*

*"Do they expect a perfect presentation?"*

These concerns are completely understandable.

Presentation exercises are very different from university assignments.

At university, presentations are often prepared over several days or even weeks.

During graduate recruitment, however, candidates may receive only fifteen or twenty minutes to review information before delivering a five or ten-minute presentation.

The exercise deliberately recreates situations that professionals encounter every day.

Managers regularly present recommendations.

Consultants explain findings.

Engineers brief project teams.

Banking graduates discuss customer proposals.

Government officers present policy recommendations.

NGO professionals report project outcomes.

Employers therefore want evidence that graduates can communicate clearly when faced with unfamiliar situations.

Fortunately, presentation exercises are not competitions to identify the most confident speaker.

Recruiters understand that candidates are early-career professionals.

They do not expect polished keynote speakers.

Instead, they look for graduates who demonstrate:

- structured thinking;
- logical organisation;
- confident communication;
- sound judgement;
- professionalism;
- the ability to explain ideas clearly.

That distinction is encouraging.

Outstanding candidates rarely succeed because they possess extraordinary public-speaking ability.

They succeed because they organise information well and communicate it professionally.

Throughout this guide, you will learn how graduate presentation exercises work in Kenya, what recruiters actually assess, how to structure presentations under time pressure, practical delivery techniques, methods for answering assessor questions and realistic examples from different industries.

Whether you are applying for a graduate programme in banking, ICT, engineering, manufacturing, consulting, government or the development sector, the principles in this guide will help you deliver presentations with greater confidence and professionalism.

# What Is a Graduate Presentation Exercise?

A graduate presentation exercise is an assessment activity in which candidates receive information about a business problem, project or organisational situation and must prepare a short presentation within a limited period.

Typical preparation time ranges from:

- 15 minutes;
- 20 minutes;
- 30 minutes.

Presentation time usually ranges from:

- 5 minutes;
- 10 minutes;
- 15 minutes.

After presenting, recruiters often ask follow-up questions to understand the candidate's reasoning.

The exercise therefore evaluates both presentation skills and professional thinking.

# Understanding Graduate Presentation Exercises

Many graduates believe presentation exercises are designed to identify naturally confident public speakers.

That assumption creates unnecessary anxiety.

Graduate presentation exercises are not public speaking competitions.

They are structured recruitment activities designed to evaluate how candidates analyse information, organise ideas and communicate recommendations in a professional setting.

Recruiters understand that graduates are still developing their workplace communication skills.

They do not expect candidates to deliver polished conference presentations.

Instead, they want to observe whether you can explain information clearly enough for colleagues, managers or clients to understand your thinking.

This is exactly what graduate employees do every day.

# The Competencies Behind Every Presentation Exercise

Graduate employers normally assess several workplace competencies simultaneously.

## Communication

Recruiters observe whether you:

- explain ideas clearly;
- avoid unnecessary jargon;
- maintain logical flow;
- communicate professionally.

Communication is measured throughout the presentation and the follow-up discussion.

---

## Analytical Thinking

Presentation exercises usually begin with information that candidates must analyse.

Recruiters therefore observe whether you:

- identify the important issues;
- separate facts from assumptions;
- recognise priorities;
- support conclusions with evidence.

Candidates who explain *why* they reached their recommendation generally perform more strongly than candidates who simply present conclusions.

---

## Organisation

Professional presentations follow clear structure.

Recruiters expect candidates to organise information logically.

Strong presentations usually include:

- introduction;
- problem definition;
- analysis;
- recommendation;
- implementation;
- conclusion.

Organised thinking often creates a stronger impression than naturally confident speaking.

---

## Decision-Making

Most presentation exercises require candidates to recommend one course of action.

Recruiters therefore assess whether candidates:

- compare alternatives;
- justify decisions;
- recognise trade-offs;
- acknowledge implementation risks.

Balanced recommendations demonstrate mature judgement.

---

## Professionalism

Professional behaviour remains important throughout the assessment.

Recruiters observe:

- composure;
- respect;
- confidence;
- time management;
- responses to questions.

Professionalism often influences the overall impression as much as technical analysis.

# How Assessors Take Notes

Many graduates become distracted when recruiters begin writing during the presentation.

They assume every note represents a mistake.

Usually, that is not the case.

Assessors record both strengths and weaknesses.

Positive observations might include:

- structured the presentation logically;
- explained recommendations clearly;
- supported conclusions with evidence;
- answered questions professionally;
- remained confident under pressure.

Less positive observations may include:

- unclear organisation;
- unsupported recommendations;
- rushed delivery;
- poor time management;
- defensive responses to questions.

Recruiters are recording evidence rather than personal opinions.

Continue presenting naturally instead of trying to interpret what assessors are writing.

Professional candidates begin differently.

# Structuring Your Presentation

Rather than speaking randomly, divide the presentation into clear sections.

| Section | Purpose |
|---------|----------|
| Introduction | Explain the objective. |
| Problem | Define the business issue. |
| Analysis | Discuss important evidence. |
| Recommendation | Present one preferred solution. |
| Implementation | Explain how the recommendation would be introduced. |
| Conclusion | Summarise the key message. |

This structure helps both the presenter and the assessors follow the discussion easily.

---

# Practical Checklist Before Presenting

Before beginning, quickly confirm:

- □ Do I understand the objective?
- □ Have I identified the main issue?
- □ Are my recommendations supported by evidence?
- □ Is the presentation logically organised?
- □ Can I finish within the allocated time?

Candidates who review these questions before presenting generally communicate with greater confidence and clarity because they have already organised their thinking before they begin speaking.
# How to Build a Professional Presentation Under Time Pressure

One of the biggest challenges in graduate presentation exercises is the limited preparation time.

Many candidates receive only fifteen or twenty minutes before they must begin presenting.

Under this pressure, some graduates try to memorise every sentence.

Others spend too much time creating notes and have little time left to practise.

Professional candidates use a different approach.

Instead of trying to prepare a perfect presentation, they build a clear structure first.

Once the structure is complete, filling in the details becomes much easier.

---

# Step 1: Understand the Objective

Before analysing any information, ask yourself:

> "What exactly am I being asked to recommend?"

For example:

- improve customer satisfaction;
- reduce operating costs;
- recommend an investment;
- prioritise projects;
- solve an operational problem.

Everything in your presentation should help answer that specific question.

Strong candidates avoid discussing interesting information that does not support the objective.

---

# Step 2: Identify the Key Facts

Most presentation exercises include more information than you can realistically discuss.

Your job is to identify the facts that matter most.

Examples include:

- financial performance;
- customer feedback;
- operational data;
- staffing information;
- implementation constraints;
- organisational objectives.

Recruiters want evidence that you can separate essential information from background detail.

---

# Step 3: Organise Your Ideas

Rather than writing a full script, organise your presentation into logical sections.

A simple structure might be:

1. Introduction
2. Problem
3. Analysis
4. Recommendation
5. Implementation
6. Conclusion

This creates a natural flow that is easy for assessors to follow.

---

> **JoinNexiva Recruiter Insight**
>
> Recruiters are not evaluating how much information you include.
>
> They are evaluating whether your audience can follow your reasoning.
>
> A well-organised five-minute presentation is usually stronger than a confusing ten-minute presentation.

---

# Step 4: Select One Recommendation

Many graduates attempt to recommend several different solutions.

Professional presentations usually focus on one primary recommendation.

You may briefly discuss alternatives, but your audience should clearly understand:

- what you recommend;
- why you recommend it;
- how it should be implemented.

Clear recommendations demonstrate confidence and professional judgement.

---

# Step 5: Prepare for Questions

The presentation rarely ends when you finish speaking.

Recruiters often ask questions such as:

- Why did you choose this recommendation?
- What risks should management consider?
- What alternatives did you reject?
- How would you measure success?
- What additional information would improve your recommendation?

Preparing for these questions strengthens both your presentation and your confidence.

---

# JoinNexiva Framework: The STRUCTURE Model

Use the **STRUCTURE Framework** whenever preparing a graduate presentation.

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| **S — Study the Brief** | Understand the objective completely. |
| **T — Target the Main Problem** | Identify the issue that requires attention. |
| **R — Review the Evidence** | Select the most important facts. |
| **U — Understand the Alternatives** | Compare realistic options. |
| **C — Choose One Recommendation** | Make a clear professional decision. |
| **T — Talk Through Implementation** | Explain how the recommendation would work. |
| **U — Understand Possible Risks** | Demonstrate balanced judgement. |
| **R — Rehearse the Flow** | Practise transitions rather than memorising sentences. |
| **E — End With Confidence** | Finish with a concise summary. |

This framework helps candidates organise presentations efficiently even under significant time pressure.

# Complete Graduate Presentation Exercise Simulation

Understanding presentation frameworks is valuable, but the best way to prepare is to experience a complete presentation from beginning to end.

The following simulation demonstrates how a graduate candidate should approach a realistic presentation exercise during an assessment centre.

Although every employer develops different business scenarios, the thinking process remains remarkably consistent across banking, ICT, engineering, manufacturing, FMCG, consulting, government and NGO graduate programmes.

---

# The Scenario

You have been shortlisted for the final stage of a graduate management programme.

You receive the following instruction.

> Your organisation has been allocated KSh 100 million for one strategic investment.
>
> You have twenty minutes to prepare a presentation recommending where the organisation should invest the funds.
>
> Your presentation should last seven minutes.
>
> Afterward, the assessment panel will ask questions.

The available investment options are:

- Digital Transformation
- Graduate Skills Development
- Customer Experience Improvement
- Expansion into New Markets

Your task is not simply to choose one option.

Your responsibility is to convince senior management that your recommendation is supported by logical business reasoning.

---

# Step 1: Read the Brief Carefully

Outstanding candidates never begin preparing slides or notes immediately.

Instead, they identify:

- the decision that must be made;
- the audience;
- available evidence;
- business constraints;
- expected outcome.

In this scenario, the objective is straightforward.

Management wants one investment recommendation.

Everything in the presentation should support that objective.

---

# Step 2: Organise Your Thinking

Before writing notes, organise the presentation.

A professional structure might be:

| Section | Purpose |
|---------|----------|
| Introduction | State the objective |
| Current Situation | Explain the business context |
| Analysis | Compare the available options |
| Recommendation | Present one preferred investment |
| Implementation | Explain how it would be delivered |
| Conclusion | Summarise the recommendation |

This structure helps both the presenter and the audience remain focused.

---

> **JoinNexiva Recruiter Insight**
>
> Recruiters usually decide within the first minute whether a presentation feels organised.
>
> A clear structure immediately increases confidence in the candidate's thinking.

---

# Step 3: Compare the Alternatives

Professional candidates compare options objectively.

Example:

| Option | Expected Impact | Cost | Risk | Long-Term Value |
|--------|:---------------:|:----:|:----:|:---------------:|
| Digital Transformation | High | High | Medium | High |
| Graduate Skills Development | Medium | Low | Low | High |
| Customer Experience | High | Medium | Low | High |
| Market Expansion | High | High | High | Medium |

Rather than selecting an option emotionally, evaluate each one using consistent criteria.

Recruiters reward structured comparisons.

---

# Step 4: Select One Recommendation

Suppose you recommend improving customer experience.

Your reasoning might be:

- immediate benefit to existing customers;
- relatively manageable implementation cost;
- opportunity to strengthen customer loyalty;
- measurable improvements through customer satisfaction metrics.

Notice that the recommendation is supported by evidence rather than opinion.

---

# Step 5: Prepare Your Opening

Strong openings are simple.

For example:

> "Good morning, and thank you for the opportunity to present my recommendation.
>
> After reviewing the available investment options, I recommend prioritising customer experience improvement because it provides the strongest balance between organisational impact, implementation cost and long-term value."

Within thirty seconds, the audience understands:

- the objective;
- the recommendation;
- the direction of the presentation.

- measurable outcomes.

---

# JoinNexiva Framework: The DELIVER Model

Use the **DELIVER Framework** whenever presenting.

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| **D — Define the Objective** | Explain what decision is being addressed. |
| **E — Explain the Evidence** | Present the important facts clearly. |
| **L — List the Alternatives** | Show that several options were considered. |
| **I — Identify the Recommendation** | State one preferred solution confidently. |
| **V — Verify the Benefits** | Explain why the recommendation creates value. |
| **E — Explain the Risks** | Demonstrate balanced judgement. |
| **R — Reinforce the Conclusion** | Finish with a concise summary. |

This framework mirrors the communication style commonly used by experienced professionals.

# Handling Recruiter Questions

After the presentation, recruiters frequently ask follow-up questions.

Examples include:

- Why did you reject the other options?
- What assumptions influenced your recommendation?
- How would you measure success?
- What implementation risks concern you most?
- What additional information would improve your recommendation?

Do not interpret these questions as criticism.

They are opportunities to demonstrate analytical thinking.

Example response:

> "Customer experience improvement provides immediate organisational value while requiring less investment than market expansion. If additional customer research became available, I would review the recommendation to confirm that service quality remains the primary issue."

This answer demonstrates flexibility without weakening your overall recommendation.

---

# Mid-Presentation Checklist

While presenting, remind yourself:

- □ Speak clearly rather than quickly.
- □ Follow the planned structure.
- □ Support every recommendation with evidence.
- □ Maintain eye contact naturally.
- □ Manage your time.
- □ Finish with a clear recommendation.

Candidates who focus on helping the audience understand their thinking consistently create stronger professional impressions than candidates who focus only on sounding confident.

# Final Five-Minute Presentation Recovery Plan

If your preparation time is almost finished and the presentation still feels incomplete, do not attempt to add more content.

Use the final five minutes to stabilise the structure.

## Minute 1: Confirm the Objective

Write one sentence explaining exactly what decision or recommendation the presentation must address.

## Minute 2: Confirm the Recommendation

State your preferred option clearly.

Remove any competing conclusions that could confuse the assessors.

## Minute 3: Select the Strongest Evidence

Choose only the two or three facts that best support your recommendation.

## Minute 4: Prepare the Opening and Closing

Your opening should explain the objective.

Your closing should restate the recommendation and expected value.

## Minute 5: Rehearse the Flow

Practise the order of the presentation once.

Do not memorise individual sentences.

Focus on moving logically from the problem to the evidence, recommendation, implementation and conclusion.

# Common Presentation Exercise Mistakes

Presentation exercises give recruiters an opportunity to observe how graduates communicate under realistic workplace conditions.

Because the exercise combines analysis, communication and decision-making, even capable graduates sometimes lose marks through avoidable mistakes.

Understanding these common mistakes before your assessment can significantly improve your performance.

---

# Mistake 1: Memorising a Script

Many graduates believe they must memorise every sentence.

This usually creates more problems than it solves.

When candidates forget one sentence, they often lose confidence and struggle to continue.

Professional presenters rarely memorise complete scripts.

Instead, they remember the structure:

- introduction;
- problem;
- analysis;
- recommendation;
- implementation;
- conclusion.

This approach creates more natural communication.

---

# Mistake 2: Presenting Too Much Information

Some graduates try to explain every statistic and every detail provided in the exercise.

Recruiters are not measuring how much information you can repeat.

They are assessing whether you can identify the information that matters most.

Professional presentations focus on the strongest evidence rather than every available fact.

---

# Mistake 3: Making Recommendations Without Evidence

Candidates sometimes announce conclusions without explaining how they reached them.

For example:

> "The company should invest in digital transformation."

A stronger recommendation explains:

- why digital transformation was selected;
- what evidence supports the recommendation;
- what alternatives were considered;
- what risks remain.

Recruiters consistently reward evidence-based communication.

---

# Mistake 4: Ignoring Time Limits

Time management forms part of the assessment.

Candidates who spend too long explaining background information often rush through their recommendations.

Others exceed the allocated presentation time and are interrupted before reaching their conclusion.

Strong candidates allocate time deliberately.

For a seven-minute presentation, a practical structure might be:

- Introduction – 1 minute
- Problem & Analysis – 3 minutes
- Recommendation – 2 minutes
- Conclusion – 1 minute

---

# Mistake 5: Becoming Defensive During Questions

Recruiters frequently challenge recommendations.

This does not necessarily mean your presentation was weak.

Assessors often ask difficult questions simply to understand how candidates think.

Professional candidates:

- listen carefully;
- acknowledge reasonable concerns;
- explain their reasoning;
- adapt when new evidence appears.

Defensiveness usually creates a weaker impression than thoughtful discussion.

---

# Mistake 6: Speaking to Slides Instead of People

Where visual aids are permitted, some graduates spend the entire presentation reading from slides or notes.

Professional communication requires engaging with the audience.

Visual aids should support your explanation—not replace it.

Recruiters want to assess your communication skills rather than your ability to read prepared material.

---

>
> Outstanding graduate presentations rarely sound rehearsed.
>
> They sound organised.
>
> Recruiters consistently remember candidates whose recommendations are clear, logical and easy to understand.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## Are graduate presentation exercises difficult?

Most graduates find them unfamiliar rather than difficult.

Once you understand how to structure a presentation and communicate recommendations logically, they become significantly easier.

Preparation builds confidence.

---

## Will recruiters expect professional presentation experience?

No.

Graduate employers understand that candidates are at the beginning of their careers.

They assess communication potential rather than polished executive presentation skills.

---

## Should I memorise my presentation?

No.

Remember the structure instead of memorising every sentence.

This allows you to communicate more naturally while adapting if recruiters ask questions.

---

## What if I become nervous?

Nervousness is completely normal.

Recruiters expect graduates to experience some pressure.

The important point is continuing professionally rather than trying to eliminate nervousness completely.

A clear structure usually reduces anxiety significantly.

---

## What if I receive difficult questions?

Treat every question as an opportunity to explain your reasoning.

If additional information changes your recommendation, acknowledge it professionally.

Adaptability demonstrates mature professional judgement.

---

## Do presentation skills really matter after recruitment?

Yes.

Graduate employees regularly present project updates, recommendations, reports and business proposals.

Presentation exercises therefore assess skills that remain valuable throughout your career.

# Key Takeaways

Graduate presentation exercises evaluate much more than public speaking ability.

Recruiters assess how effectively candidates analyse information, organise ideas, communicate recommendations and respond to professional questioning.

The strongest candidates:

- define the problem clearly;
- support recommendations with evidence;
- compare realistic alternatives;
- communicate confidently without unnecessary complexity;
- manage time effectively;
- remain composed throughout questioning.

These behaviours demonstrate the professional communication and decision-making skills organisations expect from future graduate employees.

Developing these abilities will strengthen not only your assessment centre performance but also your effectiveness throughout your career.
