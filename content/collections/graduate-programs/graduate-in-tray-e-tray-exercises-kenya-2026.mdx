# Graduate In-Tray & E-Tray Exercises in Kenya (2026): The Complete Success Guide

Receiving an invitation to a graduate assessment centre is exciting.

By this stage, you have usually progressed through several competitive recruitment stages.

Your CV has been shortlisted.

You may have completed aptitude tests, psychometric assessments or an initial interview.

The organisation already believes you have the academic qualifications and potential to join its graduate programme.

The remaining challenge is determining how you would perform in the workplace.

One of the most realistic methods employers use to answer that question is the **In-Tray** or **E-Tray** exercise.

For many graduates, this is also one of the least familiar assessment methods.

Unlike an interview, nobody asks you direct questions.

Unlike an aptitude test, there are no multiple-choice answers.

Unlike a group discussion, you usually work independently.

Instead, you step into the role of a graduate employee who has just arrived at work.

Your manager is unavailable.

Your inbox is already full.

Customers need responses.

Colleagues require decisions.

Meetings have been scheduled.

Deadlines are approaching.

Several issues appear equally urgent.

You now have limited time to decide:

- what deserves immediate attention;
- what can safely wait;
- what should be delegated;
- what requires additional information;
- what should be escalated to management.

The exercise deliberately recreates the competing priorities graduates experience in real workplaces.

That is why employers value it so highly.

Every organisation expects graduates to make decisions every day.

Some decisions involve customers.

Others involve colleagues, suppliers, projects or operational priorities.

Recruiters therefore want evidence that candidates can organise work sensibly before they become full-time employees.

Fortunately, In-Tray and E-Tray exercises are not tests of experience.

Recruiters understand that graduate applicants have limited workplace exposure.

Instead, they evaluate the judgement, organisation and professional thinking that graduates demonstrate while managing unfamiliar situations.

Candidates who remain calm, prioritise logically and justify their decisions usually perform well regardless of their previous work experience.

Throughout this guide, you will learn exactly how graduate In-Tray and E-Tray exercises work in Kenya, what recruiters assess, common exercise formats, practical prioritisation frameworks, realistic workplace simulations and proven strategies for making confident, evidence-based decisions under time pressure.

Whether you are applying for a graduate programme in banking, engineering, ICT, manufacturing, telecommunications, government, consulting or the development sector, the principles in this guide will help you approach these exercises with confidence.

# Quick Answer

Graduate In-Tray and E-Tray exercises simulate a busy working day by presenting candidates with multiple emails, documents, requests and deadlines that must be prioritised within a limited time.

Recruiters commonly assess:

- prioritisation;
- decision-making;
- organisation;
- judgement;
- communication;
- professionalism;
- time management;
- commercial awareness.

The strongest candidates do **not** attempt to complete every task immediately.

Instead, they identify priorities, evaluate urgency against importance, justify every decision and organise work logically.

Professional judgement consistently produces stronger assessment scores than rushing through individual tasks.

# What Are In-Tray and E-Tray Exercises?

Although the names differ, both exercises evaluate the same workplace competencies.

An **In-Tray Exercise** traditionally uses printed documents.

Candidates receive a physical folder containing:

- emails;
- letters;
- reports;
- meeting requests;
- customer complaints;
- internal memos;
- operational updates.

They must decide how to handle each item within a specified period.

An **E-Tray Exercise** follows exactly the same principle but is completed electronically.

Instead of printed documents, candidates work inside a simulated email system that resembles the digital workplace used by many modern organisations.

Regardless of the format, the purpose remains identical.

Recruiters want to observe how graduates organise competing responsibilities while making sensible business decisions under realistic time constraints.

Managers therefore need confidence that successful candidates can:

- remain organised under pressure.

An In-Tray or E-Tray exercise allows recruiters to observe these behaviours directly instead of asking hypothetical interview questions.

That makes the exercise one of the most practical predictors of workplace readiness.

# Understanding In-Tray and E-Tray Exercises

Many graduates assume In-Tray and E-Tray exercises are simply tests of speed.

That assumption is one of the biggest reasons candidates perform poorly.

These exercises are not designed to identify the fastest graduate.

They are designed to identify graduates who make sound professional decisions when several important tasks compete for their attention.

In the workplace, every email cannot be answered immediately.

Every meeting cannot be attended first.

Every request cannot receive equal priority.

Professional employees must constantly decide:

- what requires immediate action;
- what can be scheduled later;
- what should be delegated;
- what requires additional information;
- what should be escalated.

In-Tray and E-Tray exercises recreate exactly this environment.

# How to Prioritise Work Professionally

The biggest challenge in an In-Tray or E-Tray exercise is rarely understanding individual tasks.

The challenge is deciding **which task deserves your attention first**.

Every email appears important.

Every colleague believes their request is urgent.

Every deadline seems critical.

Professional employees therefore use structured prioritisation rather than instinct.

Recruiters want to see evidence that graduates can distinguish between activity and impact.

Busy employees are not always productive employees.

The strongest graduates focus first on work that creates the greatest organisational value while reducing the greatest organisational risk.

---

# Step 1: Identify the Real Objective

Before responding to individual emails, ask yourself:

> "What is the organisation trying to achieve?"

For example:

- protecting customers;
- meeting regulatory requirements;
- maintaining operations;
- supporting management decisions;
- protecting revenue.

Understanding the broader objective prevents candidates from becoming distracted by routine administrative work.

---

# Step 2: Separate Urgency From Importance

One of the most useful workplace skills is recognising that urgent tasks and important tasks are not always the same.

Consider the following examples.

| Task | Urgent | Important |
|------|:------:|:---------:|
| Customer complaint affecting major client | ✓ | ✓ |
| Monthly team newsletter | ✓ | ✗ |
| Compliance deadline | ✓ | ✓ |
| Routine filing request | ✗ | ✗ |
| Preparing next month's strategy meeting | ✗ | ✓ |

Professional candidates understand that interruptions should not automatically become priorities.

# Step 3: Look for Connected Tasks

Many In-Tray exercises contain documents that relate to one another.

# Step 4: Decide the Appropriate Action

Not every task should receive the same response.

Possible actions include:

- complete immediately;
- schedule for later;
- delegate appropriately;
- request more information;
- escalate to management.

Professional judgement involves selecting the correct response—not simply completing every task yourself.

# Decision Tree: Should This Task Be Done Now?

Ask yourself:

**Step 1**

Does this involve safety, compliance or major organisational risk?

↓

If **Yes**, prioritise immediately.

↓

If **No**, continue.

↓

**Step 2**

Does delaying this task negatively affect customers, management or operations?

↓

If **Yes**, treat it as a high priority.

↓

If **No**, continue.

↓

**Step 3**

Can the task be delegated or scheduled?

↓

If **Yes**, organise it appropriately.

↓

If **No**, determine the most practical completion time.

This decision process prevents candidates from treating every task as equally urgent.

Some exercises deliberately create situations where two important tasks compete for attention.

For example:

- an executive request due within one hour;
- a customer complaint affecting a strategic client.

There may not be one perfect answer.

Professional candidates explain their reasoning.

For example:

> "I would acknowledge receipt of the Managing Director's request immediately, begin addressing the customer issue because it affects external service delivery and then complete the executive report before the stated deadline."

This demonstrates balanced judgement rather than rigid thinking.

---

# Delegation Example

Suppose you supervise an administrative assistant.

One email requests photocopying for tomorrow's meeting.

Another involves resolving a supplier issue delaying production.

A professional response would be:

- delegate the photocopying appropriately;
- personally handle the supplier issue.

Delegation allows you to focus on work requiring your judgement while ensuring routine activities continue.

---

# Escalation Example

Imagine a customer reports a potential data privacy breach.

Although you may investigate initially, organisational policy could require immediate escalation to:

- your manager;
- the compliance team;
- information security.

Recognising when escalation is necessary demonstrates professional maturity.

Recruiters consistently value candidates who understand organisational governance.

---

# ICT Workplace Example

You are participating in a graduate technology programme.

Your inbox contains:

- a cybersecurity alert;
- a client reporting system downtime;
- a software release meeting;
- routine documentation updates.

Strong candidates immediately recognise that cybersecurity and operational availability represent significantly higher organisational risks than documentation updates.

---

# Manufacturing Example

Morning priorities include:

- machine breakdown reports;
- supplier delivery delays;
- quality assurance findings;
- next week's production meeting.

Professional candidates first stabilise operations before planning future activities.

Operational continuity almost always takes priority over routine administration.

---

# Banking Example

You receive:

- notification of suspicious account activity;
- branch staffing updates;
- monthly performance statistics;
- customer feedback reports.

The suspicious activity requires immediate attention because it involves customer protection and regulatory risk.

Routine statistics can wait.

---

# Government Example

A graduate officer receives:

- a request from the County Executive;
- citizen complaints regarding delayed services;
- preparation documents for next week's workshop;
- routine filing requests.

Professional judgement prioritises:

- executive requests;
- public service delivery;
- statutory obligations.

Routine administration follows afterwards.

# Decision Tree: Should I Act, Delegate or Escalate?

Ask yourself:

**Does this involve legal, compliance, financial or customer risk?**

↓

If **Yes**, consider immediate action or escalation.

↓

If **No**, continue.

↓

**Can another appropriately qualified person complete this task?**

↓

If **Yes**, delegate while maintaining accountability.

↓

If **No**, continue.

↓

**Does delaying the task significantly affect operations?**

↓

If **Yes**, complete it promptly.

↓

If **No**, schedule it appropriately.

This structured decision-making process reflects the judgement employers expect from graduate professionals.

# Measuring Success

Recruiters rarely evaluate candidates by asking:

> "Did they answer every email?"

Instead they ask:

- Did the candidate recognise organisational priorities?
- Were decisions logical?
- Was communication professional?
- Did they protect customers and the organisation?
- Could they justify their reasoning?

These questions better predict workplace performance than speed alone.

# Building Long-Term Prioritisation Skills

The abilities demonstrated during an In-Tray or E-Tray exercise remain valuable throughout your career.

You can continue developing them by:

- planning daily work using priorities instead of arrival order;
- reviewing business case studies;
- analysing operational decisions made by organisations;
- practising concise professional email writing;
- learning basic project management principles;
- reflecting on how different decisions influence customers, colleagues and organisational outcomes.

Graduates who deliberately strengthen these habits become more effective employees long after the recruitment process has ended.

# Common In-Tray and E-Tray Mistakes

In-Tray and E-Tray exercises are designed to simulate the realities of professional work.

Recruiters therefore expect candidates to make balanced decisions rather than perfect ones.

Unfortunately, many graduates lose marks because of predictable mistakes that have little to do with intelligence or academic ability.

Understanding these mistakes before your assessment significantly improves your chances of success.

---

# Mistake 1: Treating Every Task as Urgent

This is the most common mistake.

Graduates often assume that every email deserves an immediate response.

In reality, professional workplaces constantly require employees to distinguish between:

- urgent work;
- important work;
- routine administration;
- tasks that can safely wait.

Candidates who attempt to complete everything immediately usually spend too much time on relatively low-value activities.

---

# Mistake 2: Reading Only the First Email

Some candidates begin responding before understanding the overall workload.

This often results in poor prioritisation because they have not yet seen:

- management requests;
- compliance issues;
- customer complaints;
- operational risks.

Professional candidates always review the complete workload before deciding where to begin.

---

# Mistake 3: Ignoring Business Impact

Some graduates prioritise tasks according to convenience rather than organisational value.

For example:

Responding to a meeting invitation before resolving a major customer complaint.

Replying to a routine internal email before addressing a compliance deadline.

Recruiters consistently favour candidates who prioritise business impact over personal convenience.

---

# Mistake 4: Failing to Delegate

Strong professionals recognise that they cannot complete every task personally.

Appropriate delegation is often the most effective decision.

Candidates who insist on handling every activity themselves sometimes demonstrate poor judgement rather than commitment.

Delegation should always be:

- appropriate;
- clearly communicated;
- properly monitored.

---

# Mistake 5: Escalating Everything

The opposite problem also occurs.

Some graduates refer every issue to their manager.

Recruiters want graduates who demonstrate initiative.

Escalate when necessary.

Do not escalate because you are afraid of making reasonable decisions.

---

# Mistake 6: Poor Time Management

Candidates occasionally spend twenty minutes drafting one email while several critical decisions remain unfinished.

Professional time management requires balancing quality with progress.

Complete the highest-value work first.

Avoid perfectionism.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## Are In-Tray and E-Tray exercises difficult?

Most graduates initially find them unfamiliar rather than difficult.

Once you understand the purpose of the exercise and practise structured prioritisation, they become much more manageable.

---

## What is the difference between an In-Tray and an E-Tray exercise?

The competencies being assessed are almost identical.

The difference lies mainly in presentation.

An In-Tray exercise uses printed documents.

An E-Tray exercise simulates a digital workplace using emails, calendars, attachments and electronic messages.

---

## Do I need previous work experience?

No.

Graduate recruiters understand that applicants are at the beginning of their careers.

The exercise measures judgement, prioritisation and decision-making rather than previous employment experience.

---

## Is there always one correct priority order?

Usually not.

Several prioritisation approaches may be reasonable.

Recruiters evaluate:

- your reasoning;
- your judgement;
- your understanding of business priorities;
- your ability to justify decisions.

---

## What if I cannot complete every task?

That is common.

Most exercises deliberately include more work than candidates can reasonably complete.

Recruiters often want to observe:

- how you prioritise;
- what you choose to postpone;
- how you explain your decisions.

Finishing every task is usually less important than handling the right tasks first.

---

## Should I explain my decisions?

Yes.

Whenever the assessment allows, explain your reasoning clearly.

Professional decision-making involves both action and justification.

Recruiters often learn as much from your explanation as from the decision itself.

- □ Remain calm under pressure.
- □ Communicate professionally.
- □ Justify every important decision.
- □ Manage time consistently.
- □ Focus on organisational priorities instead of simply completing tasks.

Completing this checklist ensures that your preparation reflects the competencies recruiters actually assess during graduate assessment centres.

# Key Takeaways

Graduate In-Tray and E-Tray exercises are realistic workplace simulations designed to evaluate how candidates organise work, prioritise competing responsibilities and make professional decisions under time pressure.

Recruiters commonly assess:

- prioritisation;
- decision-making;
- judgement;
- organisation;
- communication;
- commercial awareness;
- professionalism.

The strongest candidates do not attempt to complete every task immediately.

Instead, they analyse the complete workload, identify business priorities, distinguish urgency from importance, justify every decision and remain calm throughout the exercise.

These are exactly the workplace behaviours organisations hope to develop through graduate programmes.

Mastering them will improve not only your assessment centre performance but also your effectiveness as an early-career professional.
