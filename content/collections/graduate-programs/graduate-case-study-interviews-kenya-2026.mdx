# Graduate Case Study Interviews in Kenya (2026): The Complete Success Guide

Many graduates expect recruitment to consist of three stages.

Submit an application.

Attend an interview.

Receive a decision.

Modern graduate recruitment is rarely that simple.

As organisations compete for the strongest graduate talent, many have introduced assessment methods that allow recruiters to observe how candidates think rather than simply what they know.

One of the most effective of these methods is the case study interview.

For many graduates, this stage feels intimidating because it is unfamiliar.

Unlike behavioural interviews, there are no rehearsed answers.

Unlike aptitude tests, there are no multiple-choice questions.

Unlike university examinations, there is rarely one perfect solution.

Instead, candidates receive a realistic business problem and are asked to analyse it before recommending a practical course of action.

At first glance, this can appear overwhelming.

Graduates often ask questions such as:

- "What if I know nothing about the industry?"
- "Do recruiters expect the correct answer?"
- "How detailed should my recommendation be?"
- "What happens if another solution would also work?"
- "Can I still succeed without previous business experience?"

The reassuring answer is yes.

Case study interviews are not designed to identify experienced managers.

They are designed to evaluate graduate potential.

Recruiters understand that applicants are early-career professionals.

They are not expecting years of commercial experience.

Instead, they want evidence that candidates can:

- understand unfamiliar problems;
- analyse available information;
- organise their thinking logically;
- communicate recommendations clearly;
- justify decisions using evidence.

These are the same abilities graduates will use after joining a structured graduate programme.

That explains why case study interviews have become increasingly common across banking, consulting, telecommunications, engineering, manufacturing, FMCG companies, multinational organisations, government agencies and development organisations operating in Kenya.

The encouraging news is that strong case study performance depends far more on structured thinking than specialist knowledge.

Candidates who analyse problems systematically usually outperform candidates who rely on instinct alone.

Throughout this guide you will learn how graduate case study interviews work, what recruiters actually assess, the frameworks successful candidates use to analyse unfamiliar business situations and practical strategies for presenting recommendations confidently even when the available information is incomplete.

Whether you are applying for a graduate programme with a commercial bank, a multinational company, a government institution or an international NGO, these principles will help you approach case study interviews with greater confidence and professionalism.

# What Is a Graduate Case Study Interview?

A graduate case study interview is a structured recruitment exercise in which candidates receive a realistic organisational problem and must recommend an appropriate solution.

The scenario may involve:

- declining sales;
- operational inefficiencies;
- customer complaints;
- expansion opportunities;
- resource allocation;
- digital transformation;
- project prioritisation.

Recruiters are rarely interested in whether candidates have encountered the exact situation before.

Instead, they observe how graduates approach unfamiliar challenges.

That distinction is important.

Graduate programmes are designed to develop technical knowledge after recruitment.

Case study interviews therefore focus on learning potential rather than existing expertise.

# Understanding Graduate Case Study Interviews

Many graduates assume case study interviews are designed to test business knowledge.

That assumption causes unnecessary anxiety.

Graduate case study interviews are primarily designed to evaluate **how you think**, not how much you already know.

Recruiters understand that graduates are entering the workplace at the beginning of their careers.

They do not expect candidates to possess years of commercial experience or specialist industry knowledge.

Instead, they observe whether candidates can approach unfamiliar business situations logically and systematically.

This distinction explains why graduates from different academic backgrounds often receive exactly the same case study.

An engineering graduate, an economics graduate and a marketing graduate may all analyse the same business scenario.

The objective is not to identify the candidate who already understands the industry.

It is to identify the graduate who demonstrates the strongest reasoning process.

## Analytical Thinking

Candidates must identify:

- the real problem;
- important facts;
- relevant evidence;
- possible causes.

Recruiters observe whether candidates:

- generate practical alternatives;
- compare different options;
- recognise risks;
- evaluate consequences.

Business decisions rarely involve perfect information.

Recruiters therefore assess whether graduates can:

- prioritise effectively;
- justify recommendations;
- explain trade-offs;
- remain decisive despite uncertainty.

- organise ideas logically;
- explain recommendations confidently;
- answer follow-up questions professionally;
- remain concise without omitting important information.

Clear communication often distinguishes outstanding candidates from technically capable graduates.

# How to Analyse a Business Case Systematically

One of the biggest differences between average and outstanding candidates is structure.

Average candidates often read the case and immediately begin discussing possible solutions.

Outstanding candidates first organise their thinking.

This allows them to identify the real business problem before recommending any action.

Professional organisations rarely reward the first idea suggested.

They reward well-reasoned recommendations supported by evidence.

---

# Step 1: Understand the Objective

Every case study begins with an objective.

Read it carefully.

Ask yourself:

- What exactly is the organisation asking me to solve?
- Is this a profitability problem?
- Is it a customer satisfaction issue?
- Is the organisation trying to reduce costs?
- Is it deciding between several investment options?

Candidates sometimes spend valuable time solving an interesting problem that the employer never asked them to solve.

Always begin with the stated objective.

---

# Step 2: Separate Facts from Assumptions

Business cases normally provide a mixture of information.

Some statements are facts.

Others require interpretation.

Professional candidates distinguish between the two.

Example:

Facts:

- Sales declined by 15%.
- Customer complaints increased.
- Operating costs remained unchanged.

Assumptions:

- Customers dislike the product.
- Marketing has failed.
- Staff motivation is low.

Notice that the assumptions may eventually prove true.

However, they are **not yet supported by evidence**.

Recruiters consistently reward candidates who avoid making unsupported conclusions.

---

# Step 3: Identify the Root Cause

Many business problems are symptoms rather than causes.

For example:

Problem presented:

> Customer satisfaction has declined.

Possible underlying causes include:

- longer waiting times;
- product shortages;
- declining product quality;
- poor customer service;
- pricing changes.

Strong candidates investigate why the problem exists before recommending solutions.

---

> **JoinNexiva Recruiter Insight**
>
> One of the most common graduate mistakes is recommending solutions before identifying the root cause.
>
> Employers are much more interested in your reasoning process than in how quickly you reach a conclusion.

---

# Step 4: Generate Several Alternatives

Professional decision-making involves comparing options.

Avoid becoming attached to the first solution that comes to mind.

Instead, list several realistic alternatives.

Suppose a company wants to improve customer satisfaction.

Possible approaches might include:

- staff training;
- digital self-service tools;
- process improvement;
- additional staffing;
- loyalty programmes.

Considering multiple options demonstrates balanced judgement.

---

# Step 5: Compare Alternatives

Rather than discussing each idea randomly, evaluate every option using consistent criteria.

For example:

| Evaluation Criterion | Questions to Consider |
|----------------------|----------------------|
| Customer Impact | Which option improves customer experience most? |
| Cost | Which option requires the greatest investment? |
| Risk | What could go wrong? |
| Speed | How quickly could the organisation implement it? |
| Sustainability | Will the benefits continue over time? |

Using agreed criteria creates much stronger recommendations than relying on opinion alone.

---

# Step 6: Make a Recommendation

Eventually you must make a decision.

Some graduates avoid committing because they fear choosing the wrong option.

Recruiters generally prefer balanced decisions supported by evidence.

A strong recommendation usually includes:

- the preferred option;
- supporting evidence;
- expected benefits;
- possible risks;
- suggested implementation steps.

This demonstrates complete professional thinking.

---

# JoinNexiva Framework: The SOLVE Method

Use the **SOLVE Framework** whenever analysing graduate case studies.

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| **S — Study the Problem** | Read the case carefully before forming opinions. |
| **O — Organise the Information** | Separate facts, assumptions and missing information. |
| **L — List the Alternatives** | Develop several realistic options. |
| **V — Verify the Evidence** | Compare alternatives using objective criteria. |
| **E — Explain the Recommendation** | Present one clear, practical conclusion supported by evidence. |

This framework can be applied across almost every graduate case study regardless of industry.

# Complete Graduate Case Study Walkthrough

Understanding frameworks is valuable, but many graduates still wonder what a complete case study interview looks like from beginning to end.

The following walkthrough demonstrates how a professional candidate approaches a graduate case study interview from the moment the case is received until the final recommendation is presented.

# Stage 1: Receiving the Case

The interviewer provides a written business scenario.

Typical information includes:

- the organisational background;
- the current challenge;
- available data;
- constraints;
- the decision that management must make.

Avoid reading too quickly.

Instead, identify:

- the objective;
- the stakeholders;
- the available evidence;
- the key business problem.

Many graduates become excited about solving the problem before they fully understand it.

Professional candidates resist this temptation.

---

# Stage 2: Organising the Information

Once you understand the scenario, organise the information before analysing it.

A practical structure includes:

| Category | Questions |
|----------|-----------|
| Objective | What decision must be made? |
| Facts | What information is confirmed? |
| Problems | What appears to be going wrong? |
| Missing Information | What would be useful to know? |
| Constraints | Budget, time, people or regulations? |

This simple structure immediately improves the clarity of your thinking.

---

# Stage 3: Identifying the Root Problem

Suppose the case states:

> Sales have declined by 20% during the last year.

Many candidates immediately recommend increasing marketing.

Professional candidates ask:

- Why have sales declined?
- Has customer demand changed?
- Have competitors entered the market?
- Has pricing changed?
- Has customer satisfaction fallen?

Declining sales represent the symptom.

The recruiter wants to know whether you can identify the cause.

---

# Stage 4: Developing Alternative Solutions

Avoid presenting only one recommendation immediately.

Instead, generate several practical alternatives.

For example:

- improve customer service;
- redesign the product;
- reduce operating costs;
- introduce digital channels;
- increase marketing investment.

Professional analysis compares alternatives before selecting one.

This demonstrates balanced decision-making.

---

# Stage 5: Comparing the Alternatives

Evaluate every option using consistent criteria.

Example:

| Option | Cost | Customer Impact | Risk | Long-Term Value |
|--------|:----:|:---------------:|:----:|:---------------:|
| Improve Customer Service | Low | High | Low | High |
| Increase Marketing | Medium | Medium | Medium | Medium |
| Product Redesign | High | High | High | High |
| Digital Self-Service | Medium | High | Medium | High |

This approach replaces opinion with structured evaluation.

Recruiters consistently reward candidates who justify recommendations using evidence rather than personal preference.

---

# Stage 6: Presenting the Recommendation

Once your analysis is complete, present one recommendation confidently.

A professional recommendation should include:

1. the recommended solution;
2. supporting evidence;
3. expected benefits;
4. possible risks;
5. implementation priorities.

Example:

> "Based on the available information, I recommend improving customer service before increasing marketing expenditure. Customer complaints have increased while operating costs remain stable, suggesting that service quality rather than market awareness may be driving the decline in sales. This option also requires relatively low investment while delivering measurable improvements in customer satisfaction."

Notice the structure.

The recommendation explains:

- what;
- why;
- expected benefits;
- supporting evidence.

# Full Case Study Simulation: From Brief to Final Recommendation

The clearest way to understand a graduate case study interview is to follow one complete example from beginning to end.

The simulation below shows how a strong candidate can analyse a realistic Kenyan business problem, compare alternatives and present a professional recommendation without pretending to possess years of management experience.

---

## The Scenario

You are given the following case:

> A Kenyan consumer goods company has experienced a 14% decline in sales over the past year.
>
> Customer complaints have increased.
>
> Distribution delays have become more frequent.
>
> Competitors have launched lower-priced products.
>
> Management has asked you to recommend the most effective response.

You have thirty minutes to review the information and ten minutes to present your recommendation.

Recruiters are not only evaluating the answer.

They are observing:

- how you identify the core issue;
- whether you distinguish causes from symptoms;
- how you compare alternatives;
- whether your recommendation is commercially realistic;
- how clearly you communicate.

---

# Stage 1: Clarify the Objective

The objective is not simply to increase sales.

The objective is to identify the most effective response to declining sales while considering customer complaints, distribution delays and competitive pressure.

That distinction matters.

A weak candidate might recommend more advertising immediately.

A stronger candidate recognises that increased marketing may attract customers, but it will not solve distribution failures or customer dissatisfaction.

> **JoinNexiva Recruiter Insight**
>
> Recruiters consistently reward candidates who define the problem accurately before proposing solutions.
>
> A strong recommendation begins with a strong diagnosis.

---

# Stage 2: Separate Facts From Assumptions

## Confirmed Facts

- Sales declined by 14%.
- Customer complaints increased.
- Distribution delays became more frequent.
- Competitors introduced cheaper products.

## Possible Assumptions

- Customers are leaving only because prices are too high.
- Distribution is the single cause of declining sales.
- Marketing is ineffective.
- Product quality has declined.

These assumptions may prove true, but the case does not confirm them.

A professional candidate states clearly where evidence ends and interpretation begins.

---

# Stage 3: Identify the Possible Root Causes

The business problem may have several connected causes.

Possible causes include:

1. Distribution delays are reducing product availability.
2. Customer complaints are damaging loyalty.
3. Lower-priced competitors are attracting price-sensitive customers.
4. The company's market position may no longer feel differentiated.

The strongest analysis recognises that the decline may not have one single cause.

---

# Stage 4: Generate Alternative Solutions

Possible responses include:

- improve distribution efficiency;
- reduce prices;
- strengthen customer service;
- increase marketing;
- redesign selected products;
- introduce targeted promotions.

A weak candidate chooses one immediately.

A strong candidate compares the alternatives before deciding.

---

# Stage 5: Compare the Options

| Option | Expected Impact | Cost | Speed | Risk |
|--------|-----------------|------|-------|------|
| Improve distribution | High | Medium | Medium | Low |
| Reduce prices | Medium | High | Fast | High |
| Improve customer service | Medium | Low | Fast | Low |
| Increase marketing | Medium | Medium | Fast | Medium |
| Redesign products | High | High | Slow | High |

This table does not produce the answer automatically.

However, it helps structure the decision.

---

# Stage 6: Select a Recommendation

A strong recommendation might be:

> The company should prioritise distribution improvement while launching a targeted customer-retention programme.

Why?

Because:

- poor availability may be directly reducing sales;
- customer complaints suggest loyalty is already weakening;
- price reductions alone could damage margins;
- marketing would be less effective if products remain unavailable.

This recommendation addresses both operational and customer-related causes.

---

# Stage 7: Explain Implementation Priorities

A strong candidate does not stop at the recommendation.

They explain how the organisation could act.

Possible next steps include:

1. Review delivery performance by region.
2. Identify the most frequent causes of delay.
3. Prioritise high-volume retail partners.
4. Introduce a customer complaint tracking process.
5. Measure improvements through delivery time, repeat purchase rates and complaint volumes.

This demonstrates practical thinking.

---

# Stage 8: Acknowledge Risks

Every recommendation has limitations.

Possible risks include:

- distribution improvements may require capital investment;
- customer retention initiatives may take time;
- competitors may continue reducing prices;
- internal departments may resist operational changes.

Acknowledging these risks strengthens the recommendation because it demonstrates balanced judgement.

---

# Example Final Presentation

> The company is facing a combined operational and customer-retention problem rather than a simple marketing challenge.
>
> Sales have declined while customer complaints and distribution delays have increased, suggesting that product availability and customer experience may be contributing significantly to the decline.
>
> I recommend prioritising distribution improvement while launching a targeted customer-retention initiative.
>
> The first step should be to review delivery performance by region, identify the most serious delays and focus resources on high-volume retail partners.
>
> At the same time, the company should introduce a structured complaint tracking process to identify recurring customer concerns and measure improvements.
>
> This recommendation is preferable to immediate price reductions because it addresses the underlying operational issues without damaging margins.
>
> The main risks are implementation cost and internal resistance, so the company should begin with a limited pilot before expanding nationally.

This presentation is effective because it is:

- structured;
- evidence-based;
- practical;
- balanced;
- concise.

---

# How to Handle Follow-Up Questions

After presenting, recruiters may challenge your recommendation.

Examples include:

- Why not reduce prices?
- What if distribution is not the main problem?
- How would you measure success?
- What would you do first?
- What information is still missing?

Do not interpret these questions as criticism.

They are opportunities to demonstrate depth.

Example response:

> Price reductions may improve short-term demand, but they could also weaken margins without solving availability problems. I would first confirm whether stock shortages are concentrated in particular regions before recommending a pricing change.

This response remains open-minded while defending the recommendation logically.

---

# Recovery Strategy When Your Analysis Is Challenged

If the interviewer introduces new evidence that weakens your recommendation:

## Step 1: Acknowledge the New Information

> That changes the situation significantly.

## Step 2: Reassess the Original Recommendation

Explain which assumption is no longer valid.

## Step 3: Adapt

Present an updated recommendation.

## Step 4: Explain the Change

Show why the new evidence leads to a different conclusion.

>
> It demonstrates adaptability, listening and evidence-based decision-making.

# Common Case Study Interview Mistakes

Case study interviews are designed to reveal how graduates think under realistic business conditions.

Because of this, recruiters often learn as much from a candidate's mistakes as from their strengths.

Fortunately, many of the mistakes that reduce assessment scores are entirely avoidable.

Understanding them before your interview gives you a significant advantage.

---

# Mistake 1: Jumping Straight to the Solution

This is the single most common mistake graduates make.

The interviewer explains the case.

Within seconds the candidate begins recommending solutions.

Professional candidates behave differently.

They first understand:

- the objective;
- the available evidence;
- the business problem;
- the constraints.

Only then do they begin discussing solutions.

Recruiters consistently reward structured analysis over rapid conclusions.

---

# Mistake 2: Ignoring Important Information

Business cases often contain information that appears unimportant at first.

Candidates who skim the case frequently overlook details that significantly influence the final recommendation.

Examples include:

- budget limitations;
- staffing constraints;
- customer feedback;
- regulatory requirements;
- implementation deadlines.

Outstanding candidates refer back to the case regularly instead of relying entirely on memory.

---

# Mistake 3: Making Unsupported Assumptions

Professional recommendations must be supported by evidence.

Weak candidates frequently say things like:

> "Customers probably dislike the product."

or

> "Marketing is clearly the problem."

Unless the case provides evidence supporting those statements, they remain assumptions.

A stronger approach is:

> "Based on the available information, customer complaints appear to have increased. Additional information would help determine whether product quality, service delivery or pricing is contributing most significantly."

This demonstrates balanced professional judgement.

---

# Mistake 4: Considering Only One Solution

Business decisions rarely involve only one possible option.

Recruiters expect graduates to compare alternatives before selecting the strongest recommendation.

Even if one solution eventually proves best, demonstrating that you considered several realistic alternatives strengthens your credibility.

---

# Mistake 5: Ignoring Risks

Every recommendation has potential disadvantages.

Candidates who acknowledge implementation risks demonstrate more mature decision-making.

For example:

Instead of saying:

> "The company should launch a new digital platform."

Explain:

> "Launching a new digital platform could improve customer experience, although implementation costs and staff training requirements should be managed carefully."

Balanced recommendations usually receive stronger evaluations.

---

# Mistake 6: Becoming Defensive

Interviewers often challenge recommendations deliberately.

This does not necessarily mean your analysis is incorrect.

Recruiters frequently ask follow-up questions to understand your reasoning.

Professional candidates respond by:

- listening carefully;
- acknowledging valid concerns;
- explaining their reasoning;
- adapting recommendations when new evidence emerges.

Changing your recommendation after receiving additional information demonstrates flexibility—not weakness.

---

>
> Outstanding graduates rarely become attached to one solution.
>
> They remain open to new information throughout the discussion because professional decision-making often requires adapting to changing circumstances.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## Are case study interviews only used by consulting firms?

No.

Although consulting firms are well known for case interviews, many organisations in banking, telecommunications, engineering, FMCG, manufacturing, government and the development sector also use business case studies during graduate recruitment.

---

## What if I know nothing about the industry?

Recruiters understand that graduates have limited workplace experience.

Most case studies provide enough information for candidates to analyse the situation logically.

Focus on reasoning rather than specialist knowledge.

---

## Is there always one correct answer?

Usually not.

Many business cases are deliberately designed with several reasonable solutions.

Recruiters therefore evaluate:

- your reasoning;
- your analysis;
- your communication;
- your recommendation.

The quality of your thinking is generally more important than selecting one specific option.

---

## Can I ask questions during the case study?

Yes, where appropriate.

Clarifying reasonable uncertainties demonstrates professionalism.

However, avoid asking questions that have already been answered within the case materials.

---

## What happens if the interviewer challenges my recommendation?

Remain calm.

Treat follow-up questions as an opportunity to explain your reasoning rather than defend yourself.

Professional discussions often involve constructive challenge.

---

## How long should my final recommendation be?

Keep it structured and concise.

A strong recommendation should explain:

- the problem;
- the preferred solution;
- supporting evidence;
- expected benefits;
- potential risks;
- implementation priorities.

This demonstrates complete professional thinking.

# Key Takeaways

Graduate case study interviews evaluate how candidates analyse unfamiliar business problems rather than how much technical knowledge they already possess.

Recruiters commonly assess:

- analytical thinking;
- structured problem-solving;
- commercial awareness;
- communication;
- decision-making;
- professionalism.

The strongest candidates avoid rushing into solutions.

Instead, they identify the real problem, evaluate several realistic alternatives, compare those options using objective criteria and present balanced recommendations supported by evidence.

Remember that recruiters are hiring graduates with potential.

They are looking for candidates who can think logically, communicate clearly and continue learning after joining the organisation.

Developing these habits will strengthen not only your performance during recruitment but also your effectiveness throughout your professional career.
